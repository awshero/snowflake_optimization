The thesis

At $40M, cost is not a tuning problem. It is a control-loop problem: measurement, attribution, detection, and enforcement, running continuously against an estate that changes faster than any human review cycle.

Every large Snowflake bill decomposes the same way. A small number of warehouses carry most of the spend; inside those warehouses, a small number of query patterns carry most of the compute; and around all of it sits a layer of idle — credits burned while the warehouse is running but doing nothing. Idle is the single largest recoverable line item in almost every estate, and it is invisible in the standard Snowsight views because Snowflake bills warehouse-seconds, not query-seconds.

The tool you are building is therefore not a "dashboard". It is four cooperating layers: a metering warehouse that reconciles to the invoice, an attribution engine that maps every credit to a team/product/pipeline, a detector library that turns the parameter catalog in section 02 into scored, evidence-backed findings, and an action layer that can actually change configuration under change control. Phases 1–3 build the first three. Phase 4 earns the right to the fourth.

Sequencing principle: fix compute first, then work down the stack

Compute is 70%+ of the bill, its levers are configuration rather than code, and most of them are reversible within seconds. Storage and transfer are structurally smaller and their fixes are slower and riskier (dropping data is not reversible). Every phase below is ordered by dollars recovered per engineering-week, weighted by reversibility — not by how interesting the problem is.

How Snowflake actually bills

Every optimization in this document is an exploit of one of six billing mechanics. Get these exactly right or your tool will produce recommendations that lose money.

The six mechanics

The unit that matters: effective cost per credit

Credits are the physical unit; dollars are the business unit, and the conversion is not uniform across your estate. List rates run roughly $2.00–$3.10 per credit on Standard, $3.00–$4.65 on Enterprise, $4.00–$6.20 on Business Critical, and $6.00–$9.30 on VPS, varying by cloud and region — and your capacity contract will have its own negotiated rate. Storage is roughly $23/TB/month on-demand in AWS US regions and higher elsewhere. AI functions bill in a separate AI credit currency, flat-priced and decoupled from edition.

Consequence for the tool: never hard-code a dollar rate. Build a rate_card dimension keyed by (account, region, edition, service_type, effective_from, effective_to) and join every credit fact to it. Two accounts burning identical credits can differ by 2× in dollars, and every prioritization decision you make depends on getting that right.

The reconciliation gate. Before a single recommendation ships, your daily credit total must reconcile to the Snowflake invoice within ±1%. Use ORGANIZATION_USAGE.USAGE_IN_CURRENCY_DAILY as the authority for dollars and METERING_DAILY_HISTORY for credits. A cost tool that cannot tie out will be ignored the first time a finance partner checks it.

The tunable parameter catalog

62 levers across eight domains. Every one is either a settable parameter, a schedulable configuration, or a design choice that a detector can observe. This table is the specification for the detector library in section 12 — each row becomes one rule.

Deep dive — warehouse compute

~$28.8M of the modeled bill. Four levers here account for the majority of recoverable spend, and all four are configuration changes measured in seconds to reverse.

W02
AUTO_SUSPEND
The idle tail — the largest single recoverable line item in most estates
Save: HighEffort: LowReverse: secondsPrize: $2.0M–$4.0M
▸
Cost mechanic
Snowflake bills warehouse-seconds, not query-seconds. After the last query finishes, the warehouse keeps running — and keeps billing at its full size rate — for exactly AUTO_SUSPEND seconds. On a 2XL (32 credits/hr), a 600-second tail costs 5.3 credits every time the warehouse goes quiet. A warehouse that goes quiet 40 times a day burns 213 credits/day doing nothing.
How teams use it
The default of 600s is inherited by almost every warehouse ever created through the UI. Teams raise it further to preserve the local disk cache and avoid the cold-start penalty on the first query after a resume — a real and legitimate concern for interactive BI.
Failure modes
  • Uniform policy. One value applied to BI, ETL and ad-hoc warehouses alike, when their arrival patterns are completely different.
  • Over-correction. Dropping to 30s on a warehouse with 45-second query gaps causes constant suspend/resume churn — each resume bills a fresh 60-second minimum and throws away the cache, so you pay more and go slower.
  • Defeated by heartbeats. A monitoring query, a connection-pool keep-alive, or CLIENT_SESSION_KEEP_ALIVE=TRUE resets the idle clock forever. The setting looks correct and does nothing.
  • Never revisited. Set once at warehouse creation, then the workload's shape changes completely and the value never does.
The fix
Derive the value from the observed inter-query gap distribution per warehouse, not from a policy document. Set AUTO_SUSPEND just above the p60–p75 gap so the common gaps stay warm and the long tails suspend. Practical starting bands: ETL/batch 60s (no human waits, cache rarely reused across jobs), interactive BI 120–300s (cache reuse is worth real money), ad-hoc/data science 60–120s, dev/CI 30–60s. Then verify: total credits must go down after the change, not just idle credits.
Detection
Compute idle credits per warehouse as credits_used_compute − credits_attributed_compute_queries from WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY, then rank by idle dollars. Cross-check the gap distribution from QUERY_HISTORY before recommending a value — a warehouse can be 70% idle and still have the right setting if it serves a genuinely bursty interactive audience.
Detector W02 — idle cost and the gap-based recommendation
-- Part 1: rank warehouses by idle credits (last 30 days)
WITH idle AS (
  SELECT warehouse_name,
         SUM(credits_used_compute)                                AS credits_total,
         SUM(credits_attributed_compute_queries)                  AS credits_query,
         SUM(credits_used_compute - credits_attributed_compute_queries) AS credits_idle
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.warehouse_metering_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day, -30, CURRENT_TIMESTAMP())
  GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT warehouse_name, credits_total, credits_idle,
       ROUND(100 * credits_idle / NULLIF(credits_total,0), 1) AS pct_idle,
       ROUND(credits_idle * 3.20, 0)                        AS idle_usd_est
FROM idle WHERE credits_total > 50 ORDER BY credits_idle DESC LIMIT 50;

-- Part 2: observed inter-query gap distribution -> recommended AUTO_SUSPEND
WITH q AS (
  SELECT warehouse_name, start_time, end_time,
         LEAD(start_time) OVER (PARTITION BY warehouse_name ORDER BY start_time) AS next_start
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.query_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day, -14, CURRENT_TIMESTAMP())
    AND warehouse_name IS NOT NULL AND warehouse_size IS NOT NULL
)
SELECT warehouse_name,
       COUNT(*)                                                      AS gaps,
       MEDIAN(DATEDIFF(second, end_time, next_start))              AS p50_gap_s,
       APPROX_PERCENTILE(DATEDIFF(second, end_time, next_start), 0.70) AS p70_gap_s,
       APPROX_PERCENTILE(DATEDIFF(second, end_time, next_start), 0.90) AS p90_gap_s,
       GREATEST(60, CEIL(APPROX_PERCENTILE(DATEDIFF(second, end_time, next_start), 0.70)/30)*30) AS recommended_auto_suspend_s
FROM q WHERE next_start IS NOT NULL
  AND DATEDIFF(second, end_time, next_start) BETWEEN 0 AND 3600
GROUP BY 1 HAVING COUNT(*) > 100 ORDER BY gaps DESC;
W01
WAREHOUSE_SIZE
Right-sizing — the lever everyone reaches for first and gets backwards most often
Save: HighEffort: LowReverse: secondsPrize: $1.5M–$3.5M
▸
Cost mechanic
Each size step doubles both the compute available and the credit rate. Cost is therefore neutral if doubling the size halves the runtime — which happens only when the query is genuinely parallelizable and not bottlenecked on I/O, a single-threaded step, or data skew. Below that break-even, upsizing is a straight price increase.
How teams use it
Sizing is usually chosen once, by whoever hit a slow query, and only ever adjusted upward. Nobody is incentivized to downsize: the person who does it owns the outage risk and none of the savings.
Failure modes
  • Peak-sized, always-on. The warehouse is sized for month-end close and runs that size for the other 27 days.
  • Upsizing to fix spilling that has a query cause. A 2XL spilling to remote storage because of an exploding join key will still spill on a 4XL — at twice the rate.
  • Upsizing to fix queueing. Queueing is a concurrency problem. The answer is clusters or scheduling, not size; size only helps if individual queries are slow.
  • Oversizing tiny work. Snowsight browsing, metadata queries, small lookups and orchestration control-flow queries all run fine on XS. Snowflake's own guidance is that larger is not necessarily faster for small queries.
The fix
Test empirically with representative queries that run 5–10 minutes, across two or three adjacent sizes, with the result cache disabled and the warehouse suspended between runs. Accept the smaller size unless runtime × credit-rate actually improves. Separately, implement time-of-day resizing (W10) so the batch window gets the big warehouse and the interactive day gets a small one — resizing takes effect immediately and only bills the incremental clusters for their 60-second minimum.
Detection
Flag warehouses where, over 30 days: zero remote spilling, p95 queued_overload_time ≈ 0, median query_load_percent < 25, and p95 query duration < 60s. Those are downsize candidates. Separately flag warehouses with sustained remote spilling as query problems, not sizing problems — route them to detector Q06, not to an upsize recommendation.

W05
Warehouse generation — Gen1, Gen2, Adaptive
A 25–35% rate increase that must be earned back in runtime, per warehouse
Save: HighEffort: MedReverse: minutesPrize: $0.8M–$2.5M either direction
▸
Cost mechanic
Gen2 bills roughly 1.35× Gen1 per hour on AWS and GCP, and 1.25× on Azure, at the same nominal size, in exchange for newer hardware (Graviton3-class CPUs, DDR5, larger cache). Break-even therefore requires a runtime reduction of about 26% on AWS/GCP and 20% on Azure. Reported gains cluster at 28–47% faster on cold runs and 15–31% on warm ones — which straddles break-even, so it is workload-dependent, not a blanket win.
Two things Gen2 gives you free
  • Query Acceleration Service on by default (scale factor 2) on new Gen2 warehouses — useful, but it does bill separately at a 1× serverless multiplier, so it is not free in the sense people assume.
  • Snowflake Optima Indexing, available on Gen2 standard and Adaptive warehouses: Snowflake watches for recurring highly-selective point-lookup patterns and builds and maintains hidden search indexes automatically, at no additional compute or storage charge. This is materially relevant to your Search Optimization Service bill (S02), which carries a 2× multiplier for the same class of acceleration.
Failure modes
  • Fleet-wide migration on faith. Flipping the whole estate to Gen2 without per-warehouse A/B evidence converts a performance initiative into a guaranteed 25–35% rate increase on the warehouses that gain nothing.
  • Migrating the wrong workloads. Sub-second point queries, metadata operations and orchestration control-flow gain nothing and pay the premium on every 60-second minimum.
  • Treating Adaptive Warehouses as a cost feature. Snowflake positions Adaptive as running significantly more queries at similar cost — throughput per dollar, not fewer dollars. Adopted without a concurrency problem to solve, it can raise absolute spend.
  • Paying for Search Optimization on Gen2 workloads Optima would have indexed for free.
The fix
Make generation a per-warehouse decision backed by a measured A/B. For each of the top 25 warehouses by spend, replay a representative 24-hour query sample on a Gen1 and a Gen2 clone with USE_CACHED_RESULT=FALSE and a suspend between runs. Migrate only where credits per unit of work falls. Prioritize the profile Gen2 actually helps: large joins and aggregations, wide filtered scans, sorts and window functions, heavy transformation, CPU-bound UDFs, high concurrency. Then, on migrated Gen2 warehouses, re-evaluate every Search Optimization configuration against what Optima now covers at zero cost.
Detection
Track a per-warehouse credits_per_gb_scanned and credits_per_1k_queries time series across the migration boundary. Any warehouse whose unit cost rose after migration is a rollback candidate. This is exactly the metric your Phase 2 attribution layer exists to produce.

Fleet topology, workload isolation and scheduled sizing
The structural lever: how many warehouses exist, what each is for, and what size it is at 03:00 versus 14:00
Save: HighEffort: Med–HighReverse: hours–daysPrize: $1.5M–$3.0M
▸
Cost mechanic
Two opposing forces. Too many warehouses: each carries its own idle tail and its own 60-second minimums, so a fleet of 400 warehouses each running 30 idle minutes a day burns 200 warehouse-hours of pure idle daily. Too few warehouses: a shared warehouse must be sized for its worst query and kept warm for its most latency-sensitive one, so the cheap workloads subsidize the expensive one and attribution becomes impossible.
How teams use it
Fleets grow by self-service. A team needs isolation, creates a warehouse, and nothing ever retires it. Meanwhile a legacy shared warehouse survives because too many things point at it to safely unpick.
Failure modes
  • Per-person warehouses. WH_JSMITH, WH_JSMITH_2, forty of them, each on 600s auto-suspend.
  • Environment mirroring. Dev and QA warehouses sized to match prod "for realistic testing", running the same hours.
  • Mixed-tenancy giants. One 3XL serving BI extracts, dbt, reverse-ETL and notebooks — sized for the dbt run, kept warm for the dashboards, and impossible to attribute.
  • Static sizing across a bimodal day. An XL held 24×7 for a 4-hour nightly batch, when the daytime workload needs an S.
The fix
Converge on a warehouse taxonomy: one warehouse per (workload class × environment × cost centre), where workload class is one of BI, ELT/batch, ad-hoc, ML/Snowpark, ingest, or ops. Encode it in the name (<ENV>_<CLASS>_<COSTCENTRE>_<SIZE>) so the metering views become self-attributing without any tagging effort. Then add a scheduled resize task on the bimodal warehouses: up before the batch window, down after it. Finally, gate CREATE WAREHOUSE behind the platform team or a Terraform module that enforces defaults and a mandatory owner tag.
Detection
Report warehouses with fewer than 100 queries in 30 days (retire), warehouses whose credits are >60% idle and whose queries all come from one user (consolidate), and warehouses whose hourly credit profile is bimodal with a >4× peak-to-trough ratio (schedule a resize).

Deep dive — concurrency & scaling

Multi-cluster settings are multiplicative on the warehouse rate. A wrong minimum cluster count is the most expensive single character in a Snowflake DDL statement.

C01 / C02 / C03
MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT, MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT, SCALING_POLICY
Auto-scale versus maximized, and the six-minute rule that makes ECONOMY worth real money on batch
Save: HighEffort: LowReverse: secondsPrize: $0.9M–$2.2M
▸
Cost mechanic
A multi-cluster warehouse bills size rate × number of running clusters. Setting MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT = MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT puts the warehouse in maximized mode: every cluster starts when the warehouse starts and runs until it suspends. MIN=4 on an L warehouse is a 32-credit-per-hour warehouse, not an 8-credit one.

In auto-scale mode, SCALING_POLICY decides when clusters appear. STANDARD starts a cluster as soon as a query queues or the system predicts insufficient resources, and favours minimum queuing. ECONOMY only starts a cluster when it estimates there is enough load to keep it busy for at least 6 minutes, and shuts one down when the remaining estimated work drops below 6 minutes — it deliberately trades queue time for utilization.
How teams use it
Maximized mode gets chosen during an incident ("dashboards were queuing at 9am") and never gets reverted. STANDARD is the default and almost nobody changes it, because the trade-off is invisible unless you are looking at credits per cluster-hour.
Failure modes
  • Maximized mode as permanent insurance. A 4× bill for a 9am–10am problem, paid 24 hours a day.
  • STANDARD on batch warehouses. A dbt run bursting 40 models simultaneously triggers scale-out to absorb a queue that would have drained in 90 seconds anyway. The DAG finishes slightly sooner for several times the credits.
  • High MAX with no statement timeout. One pathological query pattern queues, scale-out fires, and the warehouse bills at maximum cluster count for hours.
  • Lowering MAX_CONCURRENCY_LEVEL to "make things fast", which pushes queries onto new clusters and silently converts a latency complaint into a multi-cluster invoice.
The fix
MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT = 1 everywhere unless there is a documented availability requirement that survives review. SCALING_POLICY = ECONOMY on every warehouse where no human is waiting: ETL, batch, ingest, reverse-ETL, ML training, scheduled reporting. Keep STANDARD for interactive BI and ad-hoc. Set MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT from the observed p99 concurrent-query count divided by MAX_CONCURRENCY_LEVEL, then add one — not "as high as possible". Pair every high MAX with a statement timeout (Q01) and a queued timeout (C07) so scale-out cannot be triggered indefinitely by a stuck workload.
Detection
Flag warehouses where MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT > 1; where average running clusters exceeds 1.2 but p95 queue time is under 5 seconds (scale-out is firing without a queueing problem to solve); and where a warehouse tagged as batch still runs SCALING_POLICY = STANDARD.

C05 / C06
ENABLE_QUERY_ACCELERATION & QUERY_ACCELERATION_MAX_SCALE_FACTOR
Serverless burst for scan-heavy outliers — genuinely useful, and quietly additive to the bill
Save: MediumEffort: LowReverse: secondsPrize: $0.2M–$0.6M
▸
Cost mechanic
QAS offloads the scan-heavy portion of eligible queries to Snowflake-managed serverless compute at a 1× multiplier, billed to QUERY_ACCELERATION_HISTORY separately from the warehouse. The scale factor caps how much burst capacity a single query may command as a multiple of the warehouse size. It is enabled by default with scale factor 2 on new Gen2 warehouses.
The economic case
QAS is the right answer when a warehouse is correctly sized for its typical query but occasionally faces a much larger scan. Without QAS you would upsize the whole warehouse permanently to serve the outlier — paying 2× for 24 hours to fix 20 minutes. With QAS you pay 1× serverless only for the accelerated portion. It is a substitute for upsizing, not a supplement to it.
Failure modes
  • Enabled on top of an already-oversized warehouse — the outlier that justified the size is now also being accelerated, and you pay twice.
  • High scale factor on a large warehouse. Factor 8 on an XL lets one query transiently command 4XL-equivalent capacity.
  • Assumed free because it is on by default on Gen2. It is not; it is a separate metered line.
  • Left on for warehouses whose queries are never eligible — harmless, but it means the "optimization" was never real.
The fix
Use the eligible-queries system function to size the opportunity before enabling. Where QAS is genuinely paying, downsize the base warehouse one step and let QAS absorb the outliers — that is where the saving lives. Cap the scale factor at 2–4 for most warehouses. Review QUERY_ACCELERATION_HISTORY monthly against the warehouse's own credit trend: if warehouse credits did not fall after enabling QAS, you added cost.
Detection
Join QUERY_ACCELERATION_HISTORY to WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY per warehouse per month. Flag any warehouse where QAS credits are rising while warehouse credits are flat or rising — that is additive cost, not substituted cost.

Deep dive — query performance is cost

Configuration levers are finite and get exhausted in one quarter. Query performance is the lever that keeps paying, because it compounds: every second removed from a query that runs 400 times a day is removed 146,000 times a year.

The identity that governs everything in this section. For a single query on a dedicated warehouse:
credits = (credit_rate_per_hour ÷ 3600) × wall_clock_seconds × clusters_engaged
Two consequences follow. First, halving a query's runtime at a fixed warehouse size halves its cost — performance tuning and cost tuning are literally the same activity. Second, halving runtime by doubling warehouse size saves nothing. Every tuning recommendation must state which of the two it is.

The diagnostic ladder

Work the rungs in order. Each rung is measurable from QUERY_HISTORY at scale and confirmable in the Snowsight Query Profile for a single query. Snowflake's own performance guides frame this the same way — explore execution times, then optimize the query, then the warehouse, then the storage layout — and the order matters: fixing the warehouse before the query just buys a faster way to do unnecessary work.

Save: HighEffort: LowReverse: secondsPrize: $0.3M–$1.0M + tail-risk removal
▸
Cost mechanic
The default is 172,800 seconds — two days. A runaway query on a 4XL (128 credits/hr) that runs to that default consumes 6,144 credits. At an enterprise rate that is roughly $20,000–$30,000 for a single mistake, and it happens in every large estate at least once a quarter.
How teams use it
Almost universally left at the default, because nobody wants to be the person who killed a legitimate long-running job. That fear is the entire reason the exposure persists.
Failure modes
  • Unset everywhere. No ceiling on any single query, on any warehouse.
  • Set only at account level at a value tuned for the longest batch job, which leaves interactive warehouses with a multi-hour ceiling they never need.
  • Set high to accommodate one job that should have been fixed rather than accommodated.
The fix
Set it at the warehouse level, derived from each warehouse's observed p99 runtime with generous headroom. Practical bands: interactive BI 300–900s, ad-hoc 1,800s, ELT/batch 7,200–14,400s, ML/Snowpark 21,600s. Pair it with STATEMENT_QUEUED_TIMEOUT_IN_SECONDS (60–300s on interactive warehouses) so a stampede cannot force sustained scale-out. Publish the values, and give teams a documented path to request an exception on a specific warehouse.
Detection
Compare each warehouse's configured timeout against its p99 total_elapsed_time. Report the worst-case exposure in dollars — timeout_seconds × credit_rate × max_clusters × rate — because that framing gets the change approved and a discussion about query duration does not.
Q02 / Q08
USE_CACHED_RESULT and duplicate work
The result cache is free compute. Most estates leak it constantly.
Save: HighEffort: Low–MedReverse: secondsPrize: $0.4M–$1.2M
▸
Cost mechanic
Snowflake serves an identical query from the result cache for 24 hours (extendable up to 31 days on re-use) at zero warehouse credits, provided the query text matches exactly, the underlying micro-partitions have not changed, and the role has the necessary privileges. Every cache hit is a query you do not pay for.
Failure modes
  • Globally disabled and forgotten. Someone set USE_CACHED_RESULT = FALSE at account or user level during a benchmark. It is a one-line, instant fix worth a visible amount of money.
  • Defeated by a timestamp. BI tools that inject CURRENT_TIMESTAMP() or a millisecond-resolution parameter into every generated query guarantee a cache miss on every execution.
  • Cosmetic text differences. Generated SQL with varying whitespace, comment blocks or column order — textually different, semantically identical, never cached.
  • Genuine duplicate work. Six dashboards independently computing the same daily aggregate from raw events every 15 minutes. The cache cannot help across different query texts; only a shared materialization can.
The fix
First, audit for USE_CACHED_RESULT = FALSE at account, user and session level and restore it everywhere it is not deliberately disabled for benchmarking. Second, find the cache-defeating patterns by looking for high-frequency query_parameterized_hash groups with near-zero cache hit rates, and fix the generator — round time boundaries to the hour, stabilize column ordering. Third, for the genuinely duplicated aggregates, build one shared model and point the six dashboards at it. That third fix is the largest of the three and the only one that requires a conversation with consumers.
Detection
Group QUERY_HISTORY by query_parameterized_hash where executions > 500 in 30 days and the cache-served fraction is under 5%. Rank by attributed credits. Every row in that list is either a generator bug or a missing shared model.
Q03
QUERY_TAG and the attribution substrate
No cost of its own. Without it, nothing else in this program can be assigned to an owner.
Save: High (enabling)Effort: MedReverse: secondsPrize: unlocks all showback
▸
Why it matters
Shared warehouses make warehouse-level attribution meaningless. Snowflake's documented approach is two-layered: object tags on warehouses, databases and users for structural attribution, and query tags for applications that execute on behalf of many teams. QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY then gives per-query credits, which you roll up by tag.
Failure modes
  • Free-text tags. "jims test", "dbt", "prod run" — unparseable, so attribution stays broken.
  • Set at the wrong scope (account default rather than per-session), so everything carries the same tag.
  • Missing from the biggest consumer — typically the BI tool, whose service account then owns 40% of spend with no breakdown.
The fix
Mandate a JSON query tag with a fixed schema, set per session by every client:
{"cost_centre":"mktg-analytics","team":"growth","system":"dbt","env":"prod","job":"daily_rollup","dbt_model":"fct_orders","run_id":"..."}
Enforce it where the connection is created — the dbt profile macro, the Airflow connection hook, the BI service account's init SQL — not by asking analysts to remember. Then layer object tags on every warehouse and database for the structural half. Target: <5% of credits unattributed by the end of Phase 2.
Caveats to encode in the tool
QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY has real limits: it excludes idle time and non-compute costs, omits queries under 100ms, has up to a ~6-hour lag, and has known edge cases including warehouse-id mismatches against QUERY_HISTORY and inflated attribution for very short queries. Use it for relative ranking of query patterns; use WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY as the authority for absolute credits, and allocate the idle residual to tags proportionally. Document that allocation rule prominently — every team will eventually ask why their number does not tie.

Deep dive — serverless features & data layout

~$4.4M modeled. Serverless spend is the most commonly unowned spend in a large estate: it accrues continuously, appears on no team's dashboard, and is frequently enabled by someone who has since changed roles.

S01 / S02 / S03
Automatic Clustering, Search Optimization, Materialized Views
Three features, one 2× multiplier, and one question nobody asks: did it pay for itself?
Save: HighEffort: MedReverse: minutesPrize: $0.6M–$1.6M
▸
Cost mechanic
All three bill as serverless compute-hours at a 2× multiplier — an hour of reclustering costs twice an hour of equivalent warehouse compute. Critically, the charge is driven by base-table churn, not by query volume. A table that is written constantly and read rarely can generate enormous maintenance cost for almost no benefit.
Failure modes
  • Clustering key on a high-cardinality column (a UUID, an event id, a full timestamp) — near-perpetual reclustering, minimal pruning gain.
  • Clustering a table with heavy random DML. Every update disorders the table again; you pay to re-sort it, forever.
  • Search Optimization at table scope when two columns carry all the point lookups, or on tables whose queries are range scans, which SOS does not accelerate.
  • MV on a fast-changing base table — maintenance triggered by every change, exceeding the query saving many times over.
  • Stacking all three on one table, where reclustering then triggers maintenance of everything built on top.
  • Orphaned features. The dashboard the MV was built for was retired 14 months ago. The MV is still refreshing.
The fix
Institute a standing net-metering review. For every clustered table, SOS table and MV: (a) pull its 30-day serverless credits; (b) identify the query patterns it serves via ACCESS_HISTORY; (c) estimate their credits with and without the feature; (d) drop anything that is net negative. Then apply entry rules for new ones: clustering only on tables >1TB with proven filter patterns and 3–4 low-cardinality-first key columns; SOS only at column scope for genuinely selective point lookups; MVs only where the base table is slow-changing. On Gen2 warehouses, check Optima coverage before paying for SOS at all.
Detection
The three history views (AUTOMATIC_CLUSTERING_HISTORY, SEARCH_OPTIMIZATION_HISTORY, MATERIALIZED_VIEW_REFRESH_HISTORY) ranked by credits, left-joined to ACCESS_HISTORY read counts over the same window. Any object with high maintenance credits and low read counts is an immediate drop candidate — and this is usually the fastest five-figure win available in Phase 1.
Detector S01/S02/S03 — serverless features with no readers
WITH feat AS (
  SELECT 'AUTO_CLUSTERING' AS feature,
         database_name||'.'||schema_name||'.'||table_name AS obj,
         SUM(credits_used) AS credits
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.automatic_clustering_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP()) GROUP BY 1,2
  UNION ALL
  SELECT 'SEARCH_OPT',
         database_name||'.'||schema_name||'.'||table_name,
         SUM(credits_used)
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.search_optimization_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP()) GROUP BY 1,2
  UNION ALL
  SELECT 'MATERIALIZED_VIEW',
         database_name||'.'||schema_name||'.'||table_name,
         SUM(credits_used)
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.materialized_view_refresh_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP()) GROUP BY 1,2
), reads AS (
  SELECT f.value:objectName::string AS obj, COUNT(*) AS read_events
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.access_history a,
       LATERAL FLATTEN(input => a.base_objects_accessed) f
  WHERE a.query_start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP())
  GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT feat.feature, feat.obj,
       ROUND(feat.credits,1)          AS maintenance_credits_30d,
       ROUND(feat.credits*3.20,0)     AS usd_30d_est,
       COALESCE(reads.read_events,0)  AS read_events_30d,
       CASE WHEN COALESCE(reads.read_events,0) = 0   THEN 'DROP: no readers'
            WHEN feat.credits / NULLIF(reads.read_events,0) > 0.5
                                                  THEN 'REVIEW: high cost per read'
            ELSE 'KEEP' END            AS verdict
FROM feat LEFT JOIN reads USING (obj)
ORDER BY feat.credits DESC;
S04 / S05
Dynamic tables: TARGET_LAG and refresh mode
The freshness dial nobody calibrates, and the silent fallback from incremental to full
Save: HighEffort: Low–MedReverse: minutesPrize: $0.3M–$0.9M
▸
Cost mechanic
Two multipliers stack. TARGET_LAG sets refresh frequency directly — a 1-minute lag refreshes 60× more often than an hourly one. Refresh mode decides how much work each refresh does: incremental processes only changes, full reprocesses the entire result. A 1-minute lag on a full-refresh dynamic table over a large base is one of the most expensive configurations available in Snowflake, and it is three words of DDL.
The DAG effect
Dynamic tables inherit lag through the dependency graph. A single downstream table with a 1-minute lag forces every upstream table it depends on to refresh at least that often. One careless setting at a leaf can multiply the cost of an entire pipeline.
Failure modes
  • Lag set to the tightest value anyone might want rather than what the consumer actually needs.
  • Silent fall back to FULL refresh because the query contains a construct incremental mode cannot handle. Nobody checks refresh_mode_reason, and the cost jumps by an order of magnitude with no alert.
  • Lag tighter than the source's own arrival cadence — refreshing every minute against a source that lands hourly means 59 no-op refreshes per hour, each still paying to check.
  • Warehouse assignment putting a tiny frequent refresh on a large shared warehouse.
The fix
Set lag from the documented consumer requirement, defaulting to the loosest acceptable value; use DOWNSTREAM lag for intermediate tables so freshness is pulled by real demand rather than pushed by guesswork. Alert on any dynamic table whose refresh_mode is FULL and whose base is large — that is a rewrite task, not a config task. Align lag with source arrival cadence. Put refreshes on a dedicated, small, ECONOMY-policy warehouse.
Detection
Query INFORMATION_SCHEMA.DYNAMIC_TABLES and DYNAMIC_TABLE_REFRESH_HISTORY for lag, refresh mode, refresh-mode reason, refresh duration and failure rate. Flag: FULL mode on large bases; lag < 15 minutes; refreshes where zero rows changed >80% of the time.
S06 / S07
Table type and micro-partition health
Two decisions made at table-creation time that you pay for every day afterwards
Save: HighEffort: MedReverse: daysPrize: $0.5M–$1.2M
▸
Table type
Permanent tables carry up to 90 days of Time Travel plus 7 days of non-configurable Fail-safe — up to 97 days of recovery storage. Transient and temporary tables cap at 1 day of Time Travel and carry no Fail-safe at all. For any table that is fully reproducible from source — landing zones, staging, intermediate transforms, rebuild-from-scratch marts — permanent storage is money spent on a recovery capability you would never use, because you would re-run the pipeline instead.
Micro-partition health
Snowflake prunes by comparing a query's predicates against per-micro-partition min/max metadata. Files that arrive tiny and out of order produce micro-partitions with wide, overlapping ranges, and pruning collapses. This is a permanent tax on every query against that table until it is rewritten — and it also inflates Snowpipe's per-file charge and any downstream reclustering.
Failure modes
  • Everything permanent by default, because that is what CREATE TABLE does and nobody chose otherwise.
  • Time Travel set at account level for compliance on a handful of tables, then inherited by tens of thousands.
  • Streaming producers emitting one file per event, which is convenient for the producer and expensive for everyone downstream, forever.
  • Frequent small MERGEs rewriting partitions constantly and undoing any clustering that exists.
The fix
Adopt a table-type policy by layer: raw/landing = transient with 1 day; staging/intermediate = transient with 0–1 day; curated marts = permanent with 1–7 days; regulated/financial tables = permanent with whatever compliance actually requires, documented per table. Enforce it in the dbt/Terraform templates rather than by review. Separately, set an ingest contract with producers: 100–250MB compressed files, partitioned paths, batched writes. That single conversation is often worth more than a quarter of configuration tuning.
Detection
TABLE_STORAGE_METRICS gives active_bytes, time_travel_bytes, failsafe_bytes and retained_for_clone_bytes per table. Flag any table where (time_travel + failsafe) / active > 1.0 — you are paying more for recoverability than for the data. Flag any permanent table in a schema named raw*, stg*, tmp*, scratch*, or _dev*.

Deep dive — pipelines & orchestration

Pipelines are where cost is created rather than merely consumed: a scheduling decision made once bills 8,760 times a year without anyone ever seeing it again.

P01 / P02
Ingest method and file size
Snowpipe's per-file charge is fixed regardless of file size — which makes tiny files catastrophically expensive per GB
Save: HighEffort: MedReverse: daysPrize: $0.4M–$1.0M
▸
Cost mechanic
Three methods, three cost shapes. COPY runs on your warehouse — you control size, batching and the credit rate, and there is no per-file surcharge. Snowpipe bills serverless at a 1.25× multiplier plus roughly 0.06 credits per 1,000 files. Snowpipe Streaming bills at 1× plus a small per-client-hour charge and is the cheapest path for genuine sub-second row-level ingestion.

The per-file charge is the trap: ingesting 1TB as 10,000 × 100MB files costs 0.6 credits in file charges; ingesting the same 1TB as 10 million × 100KB files costs 600 credits — a thousandfold increase for identical data.
Failure modes
  • Snowpipe for predictable batches. An hourly drop that a 60-second XS COPY handles for a fraction of the cost.
  • One file per event. The default behaviour of many streaming producers, and the most expensive ingest pattern in Snowflake.
  • Snowpipe Streaming with hundreds of idle clients, each billing per client-hour whether or not it sends rows.
  • Latency requirements nobody validated. A pipeline built for one-minute freshness feeding a dashboard people look at once a morning.
The fix
Choose by actual latency requirement, in writing: sub-second → Snowpipe Streaming; minutes with unpredictable arrival → Snowpipe; predictable batches on a schedule → COPY on a small ECONOMY warehouse. Then enforce the 100–250MB compressed file target with producers — buffer at the producer, or add a compaction step in object storage before the stage. Consolidate streaming clients so idle ones do not bill.
Detection
PIPE_USAGE_HISTORY gives credits, bytes and files per pipe. Compute credits per TB and average file size per pipe; anything with an average file under 10MB is a producer conversation waiting to happen, and the report should quantify it in dollars per month so the conversation has a number in it.
Detector P02 — pipes ranked by cost per TB and average file size
SELECT pipe_name,
       SUM(credits_used)                                  AS credits_30d,
       SUM(files_inserted)                                AS files,
       ROUND(SUM(bytes_inserted)/POW(1024,4), 3)          AS tb_ingested,
       ROUND(SUM(bytes_inserted)/NULLIF(SUM(files_inserted),0)/POW(1024,2), 2) AS avg_file_mb,
       ROUND(SUM(credits_used)/NULLIF(SUM(bytes_inserted)/POW(1024,4),0), 1) AS credits_per_tb,
       CASE WHEN SUM(bytes_inserted)/NULLIF(SUM(files_inserted),0)/POW(1024,2) < 10
            THEN 'SMALL_FILES: batch at producer' ELSE 'OK' END AS verdict
FROM snowflake.account_usage.pipe_usage_history
WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP())
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY credits_30d DESC;
P03 / P05
Task execution model, frequency and failure handling
The 60-second minimum makes short frequent tasks on dedicated warehouses absurdly expensive
Save: HighEffort: LowReverse: minutesPrize: $0.3M–$0.8M
▸
Cost mechanic
A warehouse task must resume a warehouse, which bills a 60-second minimum. A 5-second task on a dedicated M warehouse running every minute therefore bills 60 seconds of M for 5 seconds of work — a 12× overhead, 1,440 times a day. Serverless tasks bill only for actual execution time and eliminate that overhead entirely for short work.
Failure modes
  • Thousands of short tasks pinned to warehouses because that is how the first task was written and every subsequent one was copied from it.
  • No-op runs. A one-minute task that finds no new rows 95% of the time, still paying a minimum charge on each check.
  • Infinite failure loops. SUSPEND_TASK_AFTER_NUM_FAILURES left at 0 (never suspend) on a task that has been failing and retrying every five minutes for three weeks.
  • Oversized USER_TASK_MANAGED_INITIAL_WAREHOUSE_SIZE so every serverless run starts large "to be safe".
  • Cron schedules that ignore reality — running every 15 minutes against a source that updates twice a day.
The fix
Route by duration: tasks under roughly 60 seconds → serverless; long, heavy tasks with predictable resource needs → a warehouse, batched with siblings so one resume serves many tasks. Set SUSPEND_TASK_AFTER_NUM_FAILURES to 3–5 on every task, as a standing policy. Gate frequent tasks on a SYSTEM$STREAM_HAS_DATA condition so no-op runs cost almost nothing. Align schedules to real source cadence. Consolidate sibling tasks into one graph on a shared schedule so the warehouse resumes once instead of forty times.
Detection
TASK_HISTORY joined to SERVERLESS_TASK_HISTORY: flag warehouse tasks with median duration < 60s, tasks whose failure rate exceeds 20% over 7 days, tasks whose runs change zero rows more than 80% of the time, and any task with SUSPEND_TASK_AFTER_NUM_FAILURES = 0.
P06 / P07
Full refresh versus incremental, and orchestrator concurrency
The largest single line item in most transformation stacks, and the hardest to fix
Save: HighEffort: HighReverse: daysPrize: $1.0M–$2.5M
▸
Cost mechanic
A full refresh costs in proportion to total table size; an incremental model costs in proportion to change volume. For a 3TB fact table receiving 5GB of new data per hour, that is a ratio of roughly 600:1. Rebuilt hourly, the full-refresh version is one of the largest single consumers in the entire account.

Separately, orchestrator thread count determines how many clusters spin up. Running dbt with 32 threads against a warehouse with MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT=6 can triple the credits for a DAG that finishes only marginally sooner — because the critical path is usually one long model, not aggregate throughput.
Why teams do it
Incremental logic is genuinely harder: late-arriving data, backfills, schema evolution, and the risk of silent divergence between the incremental result and a full rebuild. Full refresh is correct by construction. That is a real engineering trade-off, not laziness — which is why the fix requires funded engineering time rather than a config change.
The fix
Rank every full-refresh model by credits × frequency and fund incremental conversion strictly top-down — typically the top 10–20 models carry the great majority of the prize. Use streams or a reliable watermark; add a scheduled full-refresh reconciliation (weekly or monthly) to catch divergence, which addresses the correctness objection directly. Separately, tune orchestrator threads empirically: measure DAG wall-clock and total credits at 4, 8, 16 and 32 threads and pick the knee, not the maximum. Move the DAG to an ECONOMY-policy warehouse — nobody is waiting on a 03:00 batch.
Detection
Parse dbt run_results/manifest metadata (or the query tag) against QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY to build a credits-per-model ranking. Flag models materialized as table whose base object exceeds 500GB and which run more than daily. Report the DAG's total credits and wall-clock side by side per run so the thread-count trade-off becomes visible.

Deep dive — storage & data lifecycle

~$2.8M modeled. Smaller than compute and slower to fix, but it is the one domain where cost accrues silently forever and where a single account-level default can be worth seven figures.

D01 / D02 / D03
Time Travel, data extension and Fail-safe
Up to 97 days of recovery storage on data most teams could rebuild in twenty minutes
Save: HighEffort: LowReverse: data lossPrize: $0.5M–$1.2M
▸
Cost mechanic
Time Travel bytes bill at the same rate as active bytes, for each 24-hour period since the change. Snowflake stores only what is needed to restore changed rows — so the cost is driven by churn, not by table size. A 100GB table that is fully rewritten daily with 90-day Time Travel can hold roughly 9TB of history. Fail-safe adds a further 7 non-configurable days on permanent tables. Total worst case: 97 days of recovery storage. MAX_DATA_EXTENSION_TIME_IN_DAYS silently extends retention further when a stream on the table falls behind.
How teams get here
One regulated dataset needs 90 days. Someone sets it at the account level because that is the easy place to set it. Forty thousand tables inherit it. Nobody ever revisits it because reducing retention feels like reducing safety.
Failure modes
  • Account-level 90-day default inherited estate-wide.
  • High retention on high-churn tables — exactly the worst combination, because cost scales with churn.
  • Stale streams holding data extension open on tables that rewrite daily.
  • Permanent tables in landing zones, paying 7 days of Fail-safe on data that is re-ingestible from source in minutes.
The fix
Set retention per layer, not per account: raw/landing 0–1 day (transient), staging 0–1 day (transient), curated marts 1–7 days, regulated tables at whatever the policy actually requires with the requirement documented against the table. Convert reproducible layers to transient, which also removes Fail-safe entirely. Sweep for streams that have not been consumed in 7+ days and either consume or drop them.
Change control
Lowering DATA_RETENTION_TIME_IN_DAYS immediately discards history beyond the new window. This is a one-way door. Require named owner sign-off per schema, announce a 14-day notice window, and never let the tool apply it automatically — not even in Phase 4. Everything else in this document can be rolled back in seconds; this cannot.
Detection
TABLE_STORAGE_METRICS: rank by time_travel_bytes + failsafe_bytes descending, and flag every table where that sum exceeds active_bytes. Cross-reference schema naming and ACCESS_HISTORY to separate genuinely regulated data from inherited defaults.
Detector D01 — where recovery storage exceeds the data itself
SELECT table_catalog, table_schema, table_name,
       ROUND(active_bytes/POW(1024,3), 1)            AS active_gb,
       ROUND(time_travel_bytes/POW(1024,3), 1)       AS time_travel_gb,
       ROUND(failsafe_bytes/POW(1024,3), 1)          AS failsafe_gb,
       ROUND(retained_for_clone_bytes/POW(1024,3), 1) AS clone_retained_gb,
       ROUND((time_travel_bytes + failsafe_bytes)
             / NULLIF(active_bytes,0), 2)             AS overhead_ratio,
       ROUND((time_travel_bytes + failsafe_bytes + retained_for_clone_bytes)
             /POW(1024,4) * 23 * 12, 0)                AS annual_usd_overhead_est
FROM snowflake.account_usage.table_storage_metrics
WHERE deleted = FALSE AND active_bytes > POW(1024,3)
QUALIFY overhead_ratio > 1.0
ORDER BY annual_usd_overhead_est DESC LIMIT 200;
D04 / D05 / D06 / D08
Clone sprawl, orphaned tables, stage bloat and dead sandboxes
Storage nobody has looked at since the person who created it left
Save: MediumEffort: LowReverse: daysPrize: $0.4M–$0.9M
▸
Cost mechanic
Clones start free because they share micro-partitions, but they begin billing as soon as the source diverges or is dropped — those bytes appear as retained_for_clone_bytes. A nightly prod-to-dev clone that is never dropped accretes a full second copy over time. Stages bill as storage indefinitely and are invisible in table-level reporting; COPY does not delete source files unless PURGE=TRUE. Orphaned tables pay active, Time Travel and Fail-safe storage for data nobody has read in a year.
Failure modes
  • Nightly clone jobs with no matching drop. 400 dated clones of the same database.
  • *_BACKUP_20230414 tables from migrations that completed years ago.
  • Feature-branch schemas from merged PRs, and personal sandboxes belonging to departed staff.
  • Years of loaded raw files still sitting in internal stages.
The fix
Introduce a lifecycle policy with automated enforcement: every clone and sandbox object carries an EXPIRES_ON tag at creation; a scheduled task reports objects past expiry, notifies the owner, and drops after a grace period. Add PURGE = TRUE to COPY statements where the source is re-creatable, or run a scheduled REMOVE against stages. Run a quarterly orphan sweep against ACCESS_HISTORY for tables not read in 12 months — propose, notify, then drop after 30 days with a Time Travel window still open as the safety net.
Detection
Rank retained_for_clone_bytes from TABLE_STORAGE_METRICS. Diff the object inventory against ACCESS_HISTORY read events over 365 days. List stages and their sizes via LIST @stage or the stage directory table. Cross-reference object owners against the active-employee directory.

Deep dive — transfer, sharing & AI

The smallest domain today and the fastest-growing. Cortex spend in particular tends to go from nothing to a top-five line item within two quarters, before any governance exists for it.

X05 / X06
Cortex AI: model selection and invocation pattern
A 40× price spread between models, multiplied by every row you call it on
Save: HighEffort: Low–MedReverse: minutesPrize: $0.4M–$1.2M
▸
Cost mechanic
AI functions bill in AI credits per million tokens, counted on input and output, and AI credits are a separate flat-priced currency decoupled from your edition. Per-million-token rates span roughly $0.12 on small open models to $5.10 on frontier models — about a 40× spread for the same call shape. The per-model, per-function rates live in the Snowflake Service Consumption Table, not on the pricing page, so your rate card must be maintained deliberately.
How teams use it
A data scientist prototypes with the most capable available model, the prototype ships, and the model choice is never revisited. Meanwhile the function is applied row-wise inside a dbt model that full-refreshes nightly — so every row is re-scored every night, forever.
Failure modes
  • Frontier model for a classification task a small model handles at equivalent accuracy.
  • Prompt bloat. A 2,000-token preamble repeated across 500M rows is 1 trillion input tokens.
  • Recomputation on full refresh. Re-scoring unchanged rows nightly, indefinitely.
  • No per-user or per-workload quota, so a single experimental notebook can produce a five-figure day.
  • No evaluation harness, so nobody can prove the cheap model would have been fine.
The fix
Make model choice a reviewed decision with an evaluation harness: run a labelled sample through the candidate models, publish accuracy and cost per thousand rows, and default to the cheapest model that meets the accuracy bar. Persist results — write scores to a table keyed by content hash and only score rows whose hash changed, which typically removes 95%+ of recurring AI spend. Trim prompts aggressively and move static context out of the per-row payload. Apply budgets and per-user quotas on AI services, which Snowflake supports natively. Tag every AI workload so the spend has an owner from day one.
Detection
CORTEX_FUNCTIONS_USAGE_HISTORY and the query-attribution join give tokens and credits by function, model, warehouse and user. Report cost per thousand rows scored per model, and flag any workload that re-scores rows whose inputs did not change.
X07 / X01 / X03 / X04
Compute pools, replication, egress and reader accounts
Four independent meters that sit outside warehouse credits and outside most dashboards
Save: MediumEffort: Low–HighReverse: minutes–daysPrize: $0.4M–$1.0M
▸
Compute pools (Snowpark Container Services)
Billed per node-hour by instance family, from a few cents per hour for CPU-small up to double digits per hour for GPU-large, entirely separate from warehouse credits. The classic waste is MIN_NODES ≥ 1 with no auto-suspend on a GPU pool serving a two-hour-a-day workload. Fix: set AUTO_SUSPEND_SECS, set MIN_NODES=0 where the service tolerates cold start, and right-size the instance family against observed utilization.
Replication & failover groups
Bills compute for the copy plus per-TB egress across regions or clouds. The waste is scope and frequency: whole databases replicated when a handful of tables have a recovery objective, at a 15-minute cadence when the documented RPO is 24 hours. Fix: scope replication groups to the tables with an actual RPO, and set frequency from that RPO. This is usually a one-afternoon change worth six figures.
Egress
Ingress is free; egress is charged per TB when data crosses regions or clouds — unloads, external functions, external network access, cross-region shares. The waste is nightly uncompressed full unloads to a bucket in another region. Fix: compress, unload incrementally, co-locate the bucket, or replace the unload with a share.
Reader accounts
You pay for compute consumed by reader accounts you provision for partners. Without resource monitors, a partner's unbounded query runs on your budget. Fix: a resource monitor with a hard suspend on every reader account, without exception, plus a modest warehouse size ceiling.
Detection
SNOWPARK_CONTAINER_SERVICES_HISTORY, REPLICATION_GROUP_USAGE_HISTORY, DATABASE_REPLICATION_USAGE_HISTORY, DATA_TRANSFER_HISTORY, and per-account rollups from ORGANIZATION_USAGE. Each of these should be a named tile on the executive dashboard specifically because they are easy to forget.

Deep dive — account, edition & contract

The highest dollars-per-hour-of-effort in the entire program, and the part that lives with sourcing and finance rather than with engineering.

G01 / G02
Edition and region placement
A multiplier on the dollar price of every credit, applied uniformly whether or not the account needs it
Save: HighEffort: Med–HighReverse: days–weeksPrize: $0.5M–$1.5M
▸
Cost mechanic
Edition sets the dollar price per credit: roughly 1.5× from Standard to Enterprise and 2× from Standard to Business Critical, before regional variation. Region and cloud provider add a further spread of up to ~50% on both credit and storage rates. Neither multiplier is visible in credit-denominated dashboards — which is exactly why teams optimizing credits can miss a seven-figure dollar lever sitting in plain sight.
Failure modes
  • Non-production accounts on Business Critical because production is, paying a compliance premium on synthetic or masked data with no compliance requirement.
  • Feature-driven edition upgrades for one capability used by one team, applied to the whole account.
  • Analytics compute in a premium region distant from where source data lands, paying both a rate premium and cross-region egress.
  • Credit-only dashboards that make all of the above invisible.
The fix
Audit edition per account against the features and controls that account genuinely requires; the usual finding is that dev, CI and sandbox accounts can drop one tier. Every dashboard must be dollar-denominated by default, with credits as a secondary metric — otherwise the rate-card lever is structurally invisible. For placement, evaluate region consolidation as a Phase 5 architecture item with a full migration cost model; it is slow, but at this scale the annual saving usually justifies it.
Detection
ORGANIZATION_USAGE.USAGE_IN_CURRENCY_DAILY broken out by account, service type, edition and region, joined to your rate card. Publish an effective $/credit by account table. It is the single most persuasive artifact you can put in front of an executive.
G03
Capacity commitment structure
Optimizing consumption without touching the contract can save zero dollars this year
Save: HighEffort: MedReverse: contractualPrize: contract-dependent
▸
The trap
Committed capacity buys a lower effective rate against a prepaid or contracted volume. If you reduce consumption 20% mid-term, you burn the commitment down more slowly — you do not get a refund. The engineering saving is real, but it does not become a cash saving until the commitment is renegotiated or the freed capacity is deliberately reallocated to work that would otherwise have been funded elsewhere. Programs that miss this get a painful conversation with finance in month nine.
The fix
Bring sourcing in at Phase 0, not at the end. Agree up front how savings will be realized: a renegotiated commit at renewal, a reduced growth rate against a rising baseline, or explicit reallocation of freed capacity to new workloads that would otherwise need incremental spend. Then report savings in both currencies — credits avoided (the engineering achievement, visible immediately) and dollars realized (the finance outcome, which may land at renewal). Building the forecast model to support renewal negotiation is itself a deliverable, and arguably the highest-value one in the program.
Detection
Track commitment burn-down rate against contract end date, with a rolling 90-day forecast. Alert when projected burn-down deviates from plan by more than 5% in either direction — under-burn risks forfeiting prepaid capacity, over-burn risks on-demand overage rates.
G04 / G05
Resource monitors, budgets and object tags
The guardrail layer — the difference between a cost program and a cost report
Save: MediumEffort: LowReverse: secondsPrize: tail-risk removal
▸
What each is for
Resource monitors are the hard stop: a credit quota per warehouse or account with actions to notify, suspend at threshold, or suspend immediately. Budgets are the soft signal: they monitor a group of objects — warehouses, query acceleration, tables with clustering or search optimization, materialized views, pipes, tasks, alerts, compute pools, replication and AI services — forecast the spend trajectory, and send daily alerts when spending is on track to exceed the limit, routed to email, cloud pub/sub, or a webhook into Slack or PagerDuty. Object tags are the attribution substrate both of them and every dashboard depend on.
Failure modes
  • No monitors at all, so nothing has a ceiling.
  • Notify-only monitors whose alerts go to a distribution list nobody reads.
  • Quotas set far above realistic usage so they never fire, which is functionally identical to having none.
  • No tag taxonomy, so nobody owns a number and every optimization must be pushed centrally.
The fix
Deploy a three-tier guardrail. Tier 1: a resource monitor on every warehouse at roughly 130% of its 90-day p95 monthly credits, notifying at 80/90/100% and suspending at 150%. Tier 2: budgets per cost centre with alerts routed to the owning team's channel, not to a central inbox. Tier 3: a hard-suspend account-level monitor as a catastrophic backstop. Non-production and reader accounts get hard suspend without exception. Roll out monitors in notify-only mode for 30 days first, so thresholds are calibrated before anything can suspend a real workload — one wrongful suspension will cost you the program's political capital.
Tag taxonomy
Mandatory on every warehouse, database and role: cost_centre, team, env, system, owner_email, expires_on (non-production only). Enforced at creation through Terraform or a stored-procedure wrapper, with a daily untagged-object report that names the creator. Untagged objects created after the cut-over date are auto-suspended after 14 days — announced in advance, applied without exception.

Tool architecture

Four layers, built in order. Each layer is independently useful, which is what lets you ship value in Phase 1 rather than at the end.

Layer 1 — Metering warehouse

A modelled, dollar-denominated star schema over ACCOUNT_USAGE and ORGANIZATION_USAGE, snapshotted daily.

  • Facts: fct_credits_hourly (warehouse, serverless, cloud services), fct_query, fct_storage_daily, fct_transfer_daily, fct_ai_usage.
  • Dimensions: dim_warehouse (SCD2 — size, generation, clusters, policy, timeouts), dim_object, dim_tag, dim_rate_card (SCD2 by account/region/edition/service).
  • Snapshot warehouse config daily. Snowflake keeps no history of SHOW WAREHOUSES — without your own SCD2 snapshot you can never prove a change caused a saving, which makes the whole program unfalsifiable.
  • Reconciliation job asserting daily totals tie to USAGE_IN_CURRENCY_DAILY within ±1%. Fails loudly.

Layer 2 — Attribution engine

Every credit assigned to a cost centre, team, system and workload — including the idle residual.

  • Direct: object tags on warehouses and databases.
  • Query-level: QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY joined to parsed JSON query tags.
  • Residual: idle credits (metering minus attributed) allocated across tags in proportion to attributed compute in the same hour, on the same warehouse. Publish the rule.
  • Unattributed bucket is a tracked KPI with a target of <5%. It is the honest measure of whether the tagging rollout actually worked.
  • Unit economics: $/query, $/TB scanned, $/dbt model run, $/dashboard load, $/1k rows scored.

Layer 3 — Detector library

One rule per catalog row — 40 analysers, specified in full in section 14. Every finding is a row in fct_finding with a stable identity so it can be tracked to resolution.

  • Schema: finding_id, detector_id, object, owner_tag, evidence (JSON), est_annual_usd, confidence, effort, reversibility, recommended_action_sql, status, first_seen, resolved_at.
  • Every finding carries its evidence and the exact DDL to apply it. A finding an engineer cannot verify in thirty seconds will not be actioned.
  • Confidence scoring gates automation: only high-confidence, seconds-reversible detectors are ever eligible for Phase 4 auto-apply.
  • Suppression with expiry. Owners can dismiss a finding with a reason and a date; suppressions expire and re-surface. Nothing is dismissed forever.

Layer 4 — Action & guardrails

Where recommendations become changes. Built last, deliberately, and only for the reversible subset.

  • Change types: propose-only (default), PR-to-IaC (preferred), and direct-apply (a narrow allowlist).
  • Auto-apply allowlist: AUTO_SUSPEND, MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT, SCALING_POLICY, STATEMENT_TIMEOUT_IN_SECONDS, scheduled resize. Nothing that touches data. Ever.
  • Every change is logged with before/after config, an owner, and an automatic 7-day effect measurement against the pre-change baseline.
  • Automatic rollback if p95 latency for the affected workload degrades beyond an agreed threshold. This is what makes teams willing to opt in.
  • Guardrails: resource monitors, budgets, tag-compliance enforcement, anomaly alerts on day-over-day credit spikes by tag.

Build notes that save you a month

  • Latency is not uniform. ACCOUNT_USAGE views lag differently — QUERY_HISTORY up to ~45 minutes, QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY up to ~6 hours, storage metrics up to ~2 hours, and ORGANIZATION_USAGE up to a day. Build the pipeline around the slowest view you join to, and never present a partial day as if it were complete.
  • QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY has documented edge cases: data only from mid-2024 onward, queries under 100ms excluded, warehouse-id mismatches against QUERY_HISTORY, and inflated attribution for very short queries. Use it for ranking patterns, not as the source of absolute truth. WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY is the authority.
  • Run the tool on its own small warehouse with a 60-second auto-suspend, ECONOMY policy and a resource monitor. A cost tool that shows up in its own top-20 spenders report is an avoidable embarrassment.
  • Keep raw extracts. ACCOUNT_USAGE retention is finite (largely one year, less for some views). Snapshot into your own tables from day one so that in year two you still have the year-one baseline you will be measured against.
  • Buy-versus-build: commercial platforms (SELECT, Keebo, Revefi, Flexera, Greybeam and others) will get you to observability faster and typically claim 10–20% compute savings. At $40M they are worth a serious evaluation — but they cannot own your tagging taxonomy, your dbt conventions, or the incremental-model rewrites in Phase 3, which is where the largest prize sits. A defensible position is to buy observability and build the enforcement, attribution and remediation layers that are specific to your stack.

Analyser catalog

Forty analysers, derived by walking every item in the roadmap and asking: what would a machine have to measure to make this decision safely, without a human looking at it? This is the build spec for Layer 3.

An analyser is not a query and it is not a report. A query answers "what is the value of AUTO_SUSPEND on this warehouse". An analyser answers a harder question: "if I changed it to 120 seconds, how many credits would this warehouse have burned over the last 30 days instead of what it actually burned, what would have broken, and how sure am I?"

That difference is the whole design. A report tells you a condition exists. An analyser produces a counterfactual with a confidence and a guard — which is the only form of output that can be ranked against other findings, handed to an owner who will be held to it, applied automatically, and then verified after the fact. Everything below follows from that.

Six rules every analyser obeys

#RuleWhy it exists
1Emit a counterfactual, never a condition"AUTO_SUSPEND = 600" cannot be prioritized against "this table has 4TB of Time Travel". "$41,200/year recoverable" can. Every analyser models the alternative world and prices the difference, or it does not ship.
2Carry the evidence that produced the numberThe owning engineer must be able to falsify the finding in under a minute. If they cannot see the gap distribution, the spill bytes, or the read count that drove the recommendation, they will not act on it — they will escalate, and you will spend the quarter in meetings.

A warehouse spilling to remote storage is a query problem; recommending a downsize on it is not merely unhelpful, it is actively harmful. Analysers that do not cross-check produce recommendations that lose money while appearing rigorous.
4Declare confidence, and let confidence gate automationConfidence is a function of sample size, variance, and how many vetoes were checked. Only high confidence on a seconds-reversible lever is ever eligible for auto-apply. Everything else is a proposal to a human.
5Have a stable identity across runsA finding is hash(detector_id, object_id, dimension) — not a row number. Without this you cannot track resolution time, honour suppressions, or tell "still broken" from "broken again", and the backlog becomes noise within three weeks.
6Be falsifiable after the factEvery applied finding is paired to A40, which measures the actual 7-day and 28-day effect against a modelled baseline. An analyser whose predictions do not verify gets its confidence downgraded automatically. This is how the library earns trust instead of asserting it.

The shared contract

Every analyser is a function with the same signature. Uniformity here is what lets you add the fortieth analyser in a day rather than a sprint, and what lets one dashboard, one suppression mechanism and one approval workflow serve all of them.

Analyser interface — identical for all 40
analyse(window_days, scope) → [Finding]

Finding {
  finding_id        -- hash(detector_id, object_id, dimension): stable across runs
  detector_id       -- 'A02_RIGHTSIZE'
  lever_ids         -- ['W01'] -> links back to the section 02 catalog
  phase             -- roadmap phase that owns this action
  object            -- warehouse / table / pipe / task / query_hash / account
  owner_tag         -- resolved via the attribution layer; NULL is itself a finding

  observed          -- what is true now, with the metrics that prove it
  counterfactual    -- what would have happened under the recommendation
  est_annual_usd    -- (observed_cost - counterfactual_cost) * annualization
  confidence        -- high | medium | low, derived not asserted

  vetoes_checked    -- ['A13_SPILL:clear','A04_CONCURRENCY:clear'] - rule 3, made auditable
  guard_conditions  -- what must stay true for this to remain safe
  reversibility     -- seconds | minutes | hours | days | irreversible

  action_sql        -- the exact statement to apply
  rollback_sql      -- the exact statement to undo it
  auto_eligible     -- confidence=high AND reversibility=seconds AND lever on allowlist

  status            -- open | proposed | applied | verified | regressed | suppressed
  first_seen, resolved_at, verified_delta_usd
}
The field that matters most is rollback_sql. An analyser that cannot state precisely how to undo its own recommendation has not finished thinking. Requiring it at design time is what forces the author to confront reversibility honestly — and it is why the retention and drop analysers (A27, A28, A29, A30) are structurally barred from automation: their rollback is "restore from Time Travel, if the window is still open", which is not a rollback.

A01
Idle Tail Analyser
What should AUTO_SUSPEND be on each warehouse, and what does the current value cost?
Lever W02Phase 1Tier 2Auto-eligible
▸
Decision
Sets the idle tail per warehouse. This matters more than any other single setting because Snowflake bills warehouse-seconds rather than query-seconds: the tail is pure loss, it is invisible in every default view, and at $40M scale it is typically the largest recoverable line in the estate.
Reads
WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY (credits_used_compute, credits_attributed_compute_queries), QUERY_HISTORY (start_time, end_time per warehouse), WAREHOUSE_EVENTS_HISTORY (actual suspend/resume), current config snapshot.
Method
Builds the inter-query gap distribution per warehouse over 14 days. Then it replays the query timeline against candidate values — 30, 60, 120, 300, 600s — simulating for each: seconds the warehouse would have stayed running, how many additional resumes it would have incurred, and the 60-second minimum charged on each of those resumes. Cost for each candidate is (running_seconds + extra_resumes × 60) × size_rate. It recommends the cheapest candidate whose extra resume count stays within a cache-preservation tolerance, and prices the delta against actual metered credits.
Fires when
Modelled saving exceeds $5k/year and the recommended value differs from current by at least one band. Starting bands by workload class: ETL/batch 60s, interactive BI 120–300s, ad-hoc 60–120s, dev/CI 30–60s — but the replay overrides the band whenever it disagrees.
Guards
Veto from A10. If a heartbeat keeps the warehouse alive, the gap distribution is a lie and no value will help — the finding is rewritten as an A10 finding instead. Cache-cliff guard: reject any candidate that more than doubles resume count on a warehouse where percentage_scanned_from_cache is materially above the estate median; on those, warm cache is worth real money. Sample floor: at least 100 gaps, or confidence drops to low.
Emits
Per warehouse: current value, recommended value, modelled 30-day credits at each candidate, extra resumes implied, $/year delta. ALTER WAREHOUSE … SET AUTO_SUSPEND = n plus the rollback. Auto-eligible at high confidence.
A02
Right-Size Analyser
Is this warehouse one or more sizes larger than its workload needs?
Lever W01Phase 1Tier 2Auto-eligible (one step)
▸
Decision
Recommends a size change. It is the highest-value and highest-risk analyser in the library, because each size step doubles the credit rate — and because a naive version of it is the classic way a cost program causes its first incident.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (warehouse_size, total_elapsed_time, execution_time, query_load_percent, bytes_spilled_to_local_storage, bytes_spilled_to_remote_storage, queued_overload_time), WAREHOUSE_LOAD_HISTORY, WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY.
Method
Establishes headroom from four independent signals: no remote spill (memory headroom), no local spill growth (memory margin), p95 queue ≈ 0 (concurrency headroom), and median query_load_percent well below saturation (CPU headroom). Where all four hold, the workload is not consuming the resources it is paying for. The counterfactual assumes a pessimistic scaling penalty: model runtime at the next size down as runtime × 1.8, not × 2 — deliberately conservative, since if a 1.8× slowdown still costs less than the current size (and it does, because the rate halves), the recommendation survives its own worst case.
Fires when
Remote spill = 0 and p95 queued_overload_time < 2s and mean query_load_percent < 25 and p95 runtime < 60s and > 500 queries in the window. Recommends exactly one step per cycle — never two, regardless of how much headroom it sees.
Guards
Veto from A13 (spill) — hard block, no exceptions: a spilling warehouse is routed to Phase 3 as a query problem. Veto from A04 (concurrency) — queueing is solved with clusters, not size. Latency-SLA guard: warehouses tagged with a documented p95 SLA require human approval. One-step rule exists because scaling is non-linear and unobserved below the current size — you are extrapolating, so take one step and re-measure with A40 rather than trusting the model twice.
Emits
Current size, recommended size, the four headroom signals with values, modelled credits at the smaller size under the pessimistic penalty, $/year delta, and the explicit list of vetoes checked and cleared. Auto-eligible for a single step at high confidence; two or more steps always require a human.
A03
Cluster Elasticity Analyser
Are the multi-cluster settings buying anything, or multiplying the bill?
Levers C01 C02 C03Phase 1Tier 2Auto-eligible
▸
Decision
Sets MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT, MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT and SCALING_POLICY. Cluster count is a straight multiplier on the warehouse rate, which makes MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT the most expensive single character in a Snowflake DDL statement — and it is routinely set during an incident and never reverted.
Reads
WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY (hourly credits ÷ size rate implies clusters running), WAREHOUSE_LOAD_HISTORY (avg_running, avg_queued_load), QUERY_HISTORY (queued_overload_time, concurrent-query counts by second), config snapshot, workload-class tag.
Method
Three independent tests. (a) Minimum: derive clusters actually needed per hour from concurrent-query count ÷ MAX_CONCURRENCY_LEVEL; if the p95 hour needs one cluster, everything above MIN=1 is waste, priced directly. (b) Maximum: set from observed p99 concurrency ÷ concurrency level, plus one for headroom; a ceiling far above that is unpriced tail risk rather than current cost, so it is emitted as a risk finding, not a savings finding. (c) Policy: for warehouses with no interactive consumer, simulate ECONOMY's six-minute rule against the observed arrival pattern — count bursts that would have drained within six minutes and therefore never triggered a new cluster, and price the clusters not started.
Fires when
MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT > 1 with p95 hourly cluster demand of 1; or SCALING_POLICY = STANDARD on a warehouse whose workload class is batch/ELT/ingest and where average running clusters exceeds 1.2 while under 1% of queries ever queued; or MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT more than 3× observed p99 need.
Guards
Veto from A04: a warehouse with genuine sustained queueing keeps its elasticity. Interactive guard: ECONOMY is never recommended for a warehouse whose queries originate from a BI tool or a human-facing role — the six-minute rule trades latency for utilization, and that trade is only acceptable when nobody is waiting. HA exemption: honour a documented availability tag on MIN_CLUSTER_COUNT, and surface the annual cost of that exemption so the decision is re-made consciously each year rather than inherited.
Emits
Three separate findings per warehouse so each can be actioned and suppressed independently, each with modelled cluster-hours avoided and $/year. Auto-eligible for MIN and policy at high confidence; MAX changes are proposals.
A04
Concurrency Saturation Analyser
Is queueing real, and is it a size problem, a cluster problem, or a scheduling problem?
Levers C04 C07Phase 1–2Tier 1Veto provider
▸
Decision
Classifies every warehouse's queueing behaviour. Its primary product is not a saving — it is the veto that stops A02 and A03 from shrinking a warehouse that is genuinely under pressure. It also recommends MAX_CONCURRENCY_LEVEL and STATEMENT_QUEUED_TIMEOUT_IN_SECONDS.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (queued_overload_time, queued_provisioning_time, queued_repair_time, compilation_time), WAREHOUSE_LOAD_HISTORY (avg_queued_load, avg_running).
Method
Separates the three queue types, which have completely different remedies and are routinely conflated. Overload queueing means the cluster is saturated → clusters or concurrency level. Provisioning queueing means the warehouse was resuming → an auto-suspend or scheduling matter, and often an argument against an aggressive A01 recommendation. Repair queueing is infrastructure and is informational only. It then profiles queueing by hour-of-week to distinguish a genuine sustained concurrency problem from a narrow daily spike that scheduling would fix more cheaply than capacity.
Fires when
p95 overload queue > 30s on an interactive warehouse (capacity finding); queueing concentrated in <5% of hours (scheduling finding, materially cheaper); MAX_CONCURRENCY_LEVEL below default with no spill evidence justifying it; queued timeout unset on a warehouse with a high maximum cluster count (tail-risk finding).
Guards
Excludes queries whose queue time is dominated by provisioning — counting those as concurrency pressure is the most common way to talk yourself into an unnecessary upsize. Ignores warehouses below a minimum query volume, where p95 is statistically meaningless.
Emits
A per-warehouse classification (saturated / spiky / healthy) consumed as a veto by A02 and A03, plus its own findings on concurrency level and queued timeout.
A05
Load Bimodality Analyser
Does this warehouse need two different sizes at two different times of day?
Lever W10Phase 2Tier 1Proposal
▸
Decision
Identifies warehouses whose demand profile is strongly bimodal and proposes a scheduled resize. This is the lever that rescues the workloads A02 cannot help: a warehouse genuinely needs an XL for its four-hour batch window, so A02 correctly refuses to downsize it — and it then burns XL rates for the other twenty hours.
Reads
WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY hourly, QUERY_HISTORY aggregated to hour-of-week, WAREHOUSE_LOAD_HISTORY.
Method
Builds a 168-bucket hour-of-week demand profile from concurrent load, bytes scanned and spill. Tests for bimodality by comparing peak-window demand against the trough-window median. Where the ratio is large and the windows are stable week over week, it proposes a two-state schedule and prices it: trough_hours × (current_rate − proposed_rate), minus the resize overhead, which is small because resizing bills only the incremental clusters for their 60-second minimum.
Fires when
Peak-to-trough demand ratio > 4×, the pattern holds in at least 3 of 4 observed weeks, and the modelled saving exceeds $10k/year.
Guards
Stability requirement: a profile that shifts week to week produces a schedule that will be wrong, so the multi-week consistency test is mandatory. Boundary guard: proposed transitions must sit at least 30 minutes clear of the observed peak edges, so a batch that starts early is not throttled. Overrun guard: the schedule must be paired with a monitor that alerts if the batch is still running when the downsize fires — and the recommended implementation resizes on batch completion rather than on a clock where the orchestrator can signal it.
Emits
Proposed schedule with the hour-of-week profile as evidence, the two sizes, modelled $/year, and the task DDL implementing both transitions. Always a proposal — the failure mode is a throttled batch, which is a business impact rather than a cost one.
A06
Fleet Topology Analyser
Which warehouses should be retired, merged, or split apart?
Levers W08 W09 W11Phase 1–5Tier 2Proposal
▸
Decision
Operates on the fleet rather than the warehouse. Each warehouse carries its own idle tail and its own 60-second minimums, so fleet size is itself a cost driver — but consolidating too far creates warehouses that must be sized for their worst query and kept warm for their most impatient consumer. This analyser finds both errors.
Reads
WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY, QUERY_HISTORY (distinct users, roles, query shapes, arrival times), ACCESS_HISTORY (object overlap between warehouses), tags, config snapshot.
Method
Three passes. Retire: warehouses under a query-volume floor, or with credits but effectively no useful work. Consolidate: cluster warehouses by similarity of workload class, arrival profile, size and object footprint; where two warehouses in the same class have non-overlapping active hours, model the merged idle tail — the saving is the tail eliminated, not the compute. Split: the inverse test — compute a tenancy-mixing score from the variance of query shapes on one warehouse (interactive sub-second queries sharing with multi-hour batch), because that mixture is what forces oversizing and destroys attribution.
Fires when
Retire: <100 queries in 30 days. Consolidate: same class, <20% active-hour overlap, combined credits > threshold. Split: tenancy-mixing score above threshold on a warehouse in the top 20 by spend.
Guards
Blast radius: consolidation changes connection strings and breaks things in ways config changes do not — it is always a proposal with a migration plan, never automated. Isolation intent: honour a tag declaring deliberate isolation (compliance, tenancy, chargeback). Seasonality: a 30-day window will flag a quarter-end warehouse as dead — retirement requires a 90-day window and an owner ping before any drop.
Emits
A target-state fleet map with per-move rationale and $/year, feeding the Phase 5 topology work. Retirement findings are actionable in Phase 1; consolidation and splits are Phase 5.
A07
Generation Economics Analyser
Does Gen2 pay for itself on this warehouse — and should Adaptive be considered at all?
Levers W05 W07Phase 2Tier 2Proposal
▸
Decision
Makes generation a per-warehouse economic decision instead of an estate-wide article of faith. Gen2 bills roughly 1.35× Gen1 on AWS/GCP and 1.25× on Azure, so break-even requires a ~26% / ~20% runtime reduction. Observed gains straddle that line, which means a fleet-wide migration is guaranteed to raise cost on some meaningful fraction of the estate.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY with query_parameterized_hash, bytes_scanned, execution_time; WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY; config snapshot including generation; the A/B replay harness results.
Method
Two modes. Pre-migration: profile the warehouse's workload against the shapes Gen2 demonstrably helps — large joins and aggregations, wide filtered scans, sorts and window functions, CPU-bound UDFs, high concurrency — versus those it does not: sub-second point queries, metadata operations, very short statements. Produces a predicted gain and a recommendation to A/B or skip. Post-migration: the real test — track credits per GB scanned and credits per 1,000 executions of the same parameterized hash across the migration boundary. Same work, same unit, different generation. Any warehouse whose unit cost rose is a rollback finding.
Fires when
Predicted gain below break-even → do not migrate. Post-migration unit cost up >5% with a stable workload mix → roll back. Adaptive proposed anywhere without a documented concurrency problem → challenge finding, since Adaptive is throughput-per-dollar and not spend reduction.
Guards
Workload-mix drift: a unit-cost rise means nothing if the query mix changed at the same time — the comparison is restricted to parameterized hashes present on both sides of the boundary, with similar volumes. Cache state: A/B replays must run with USE_CACHED_RESULT = FALSE and a suspend between runs, or the second generation tested always looks faster. Optima interaction: on Gen2, part of the gain may come from Optima Indexing building hidden indexes on recurring point lookups at no charge — which is real, but it means the same workload may also no longer need paid Search Optimization, and that is a separate A19 finding.
Emits
Per warehouse: predicted or measured unit-cost delta, workload-shape profile, migrate/hold/rollback verdict, $/year. Never automated — generation changes are a fleet-level decision with a performance story attached.
A08
Query Acceleration Net-Value Analyser
Is QAS substituting for warehouse size, or just adding a second bill?
Levers C05 C06Phase 2Tier 2Proposal
▸
Decision
Decides whether QAS is enabled, at what scale factor, and — the part that actually saves money — whether the base warehouse can now come down a size. QAS is default-on with scale factor 2 for new Gen2 warehouses, which means many teams are paying for it without ever having decided to.
Reads
QUERY_ACCELERATION_HISTORY, WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY, QUERY_HISTORY (eligible query shapes, scan skew), the QAS eligible-queries system function, config snapshot.
Method
The core insight is that QAS is a substitute for permanent upsizing, not a supplement to it. So the analyser tests the pairing rather than the feature: it measures scan-volume skew (p99 bytes scanned ÷ median) to identify warehouses correctly sized for typical work but occasionally meeting a much larger scan — the profile QAS exists for — then models total cost as warehouse credits at one size down + QAS credits at 1× against warehouse credits at the current size. Where QAS is already on, it runs the same comparison retrospectively: did warehouse credits actually fall after enablement, or did QAS credits simply get added on top?
Fires when
High scan skew with no QAS → propose enable + downsize as a single paired change. QAS credits rising while warehouse credits flat or rising → additive-cost finding. Scale factor > 4 → cap finding, since a factor of 8 on an XL lets one query transiently command 4XL-equivalent capacity.
Guards
Paired-change rule: never emit "enable QAS" alone. Unpaired, it is a guaranteed cost increase — the saving lives entirely in the downsize it makes safe. Eligibility floor: if few queries are QAS-eligible, the feature will do nothing and the finding is suppressed rather than shipped as a non-event.
Emits
A single paired finding (enable + downsize, or disable), with the eligibility profile, the skew statistic, both cost models, and $/year. Proposal only — it changes two things at once, which is exactly the class of change a human should approve.
A09
Warehouse Type Fit Analyser
Does this Snowpark-optimized warehouse actually need the memory it is paying ~1.5× for?
Lever W06Phase 2Tier 2Proposal
▸
Decision
Reverts Snowpark-optimized warehouses to standard where the memory ceiling is never approached. Snowpark-optimized runs about 1.5× the standard rate at the same size — a Medium costs 6 credits/hour rather than 4 — and it very often becomes a team's blanket default for anything written in Python.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (spill, elapsed time, query type), Snowpark and stored-procedure execution metadata, config snapshot (WAREHOUSE_TYPE, RESOURCE_CONSTRAINT), WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY.
Method
Uses spill as the memory-pressure proxy, since it is the observable signal that a workload exceeded available memory. A Snowpark-optimized warehouse with zero remote spill and negligible local spill across a full window has never used the headroom it is paying a permanent premium for. Prices the counterfactual at the standard rate for the same size and hours. Where memory pressure is real but intermittent, it proposes splitting the workload instead: the memory-hungry jobs onto a small Snowpark-optimized warehouse, the rest onto standard.
Fires when
Zero remote spill and local spill below a low threshold over 30 days on a Snowpark-optimized warehouse, with meaningful credits. Or: memory pressure confined to a small minority of jobs → split finding.
Guards
Failure-mode asymmetry: insufficient memory produces hard job failures, not gentle degradation, so this analyser is deliberately conservative — it requires a full window with no remote spill at all, and it never recommends a type change and a size change in the same cycle. Job coverage: if the window does not contain at least one full monthly cycle of the relevant jobs, confidence drops to low.
Emits
Type-revert or workload-split proposal with spill evidence and $/year. Proposal only.
A10
Session Liveness Analyser
What is keeping this warehouse awake when nobody is using it?
Levers Q04 W03Phase 1Tier 1Veto provider
▸
Decision
Finds the traffic that prevents auto-suspend from ever firing. This is a small analyser with outsized importance, because it is the reason A01's recommendations sometimes produce no saving at all — and if you cannot explain that, the whole library loses credibility on first contact.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (query_text, user_name, session_id, start_time, bytes_scanned), SESSIONS, LOGIN_HISTORY, config snapshot for AUTO_SUSPEND.
Method
Looks for metronomic traffic: queries from a single service account arriving at a near-constant interval shorter than the warehouse's AUTO_SUSPEND, scanning almost nothing, and often textually identical (SELECT 1, SELECT CURRENT_VERSION(), a trivial count, a driver keep-alive). Low interval variance is the giveaway — humans are not metronomes. It then measures the suspend-starvation cost: hours in which the warehouse never suspended and the only traffic was heartbeats, priced at the full warehouse rate.
Fires when
Interval variance below threshold, mean gap < AUTO_SUSPEND, bytes scanned near zero, and more than a few such hours per week.
Guards
Legitimate monitoring: a genuine health check is fine — the recommendation is to point it at a dedicated XS warehouse or replace it with a metadata call, not to delete it. Attribution before action: the finding must name the offending user, application and session, because the fix lives in a connection pool configuration in someone else's repository, not in Snowflake.
Emits
Named source, cadence, starvation hours, $/year, and the concrete remediation (disable CLIENT_SESSION_KEEP_ALIVE, lengthen the poll, or move it). Also emits a veto flag to A01, which is its most valuable output.

B · Query analysers

These produce pull requests, not ALTER statements. Their findings are slower to land and none of them are automatable — but they are the only findings that compound, because a query pattern fixed here stays fixed as volume grows.

A11
Workload Census
What are the hundred things this account actually spends its money doing?
FoundationalPhase 1Tier 0Input to everything
▸
Decision
Decides what is worth analysing at all. At $40M scale the estate contains millions of distinct queries and no team can look at them. The census collapses them into a few hundred patterns and ranks those by cost, which is what makes every downstream query analyser tractable instead of a search problem.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (query_parameterized_hash, query_hash, all profile metrics), QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY (credits_attributed_compute, credits_used_query_acceleration), tags.
Method
Groups by query_parameterized_hash — structurally identical queries whose literals differ — which is the correct unit of optimization, because fixing one pattern fixes every execution of it. For each pattern it aggregates attributed credits, executions, runtime percentiles, pruning ratio, spill, cache rate and owning tag. Crucially it ranks on total cost, not unit cost: the pattern that costs $0.40 and runs 900,000 times outranks the one that costs $90 and runs twice, and human attention naturally goes to the wrong one.
Fires when
Always — it is a ranked inventory, not a rule. It emits findings only for patterns above a materiality floor whose diagnostics are abnormal, delegating the diagnosis to A12–A16.
Guards
Attribution caveats, encoded: QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY excludes idle and non-compute cost, omits sub-100ms queries, lags up to six hours, and has documented edge cases including warehouse-id mismatches and inflated attribution for very short queries. So the census uses it for relative ranking only and reconciles totals to WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY. Publishing an absolute per-query dollar figure sourced only from attribution will eventually be challenged and will not survive.
Emits
The ranked pattern table with diagnostics attached — the shared input for A12 through A16, and the single most-used artifact in the whole platform.
A12
Pruning Analyser
Why is this query reading the whole table when it asked for one day?
Lever Q05Phase 3Tier 1Veto provider
▸
Decision
Identifies patterns whose predicates cannot be used for micro-partition elimination. Pruning failure is the most cost-multiplying defect in Snowflake — credits scale with partitions scanned, so a query reading 100% of a table instead of 2% costs fifty times what it should, on every execution, forever.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (partitions_scanned, partitions_total, bytes_scanned, query_text), ACCESS_HISTORY (columns actually filtered), SYSTEM$CLUSTERING_INFORMATION for the target tables.
Method
Computes the prune ratio per pattern, then — and this is what makes it useful rather than merely alarming — attributes the cause. It parses the predicate and classifies: a function or cast wrapping the filtered column (TO_DATE(ts) = …), a type-mismatched join key, a filter on a column the table has no useful ordering for, or a genuinely unselective predicate where a full scan is correct. Each cause has a different remedy, and the counterfactual differs accordingly: a rewritable predicate is modelled at the table's achievable prune ratio; a layout problem is handed to A20.
Fires when
Prune ratio > 0.8 on tables above a partition floor, more than 20 executions in the window, and a classified cause. Patterns with no identifiable cause are reported as unclassified rather than guessed at.
Guards
Legitimate full scans exist — a nightly full aggregate genuinely reads everything, and flagging it is noise that teaches people to ignore the tool. Suppress where the query is an unfiltered aggregate. Do not conflate causes: a rewritable predicate is a free fix; a layout problem costs 2×-multiplier reclustering, and recommending clustering for a problem a WHERE clause rewrite would solve is an expensive mistake.
Emits
Pattern, target table, prune ratio, classified cause, suggested rewrite, modelled $/year. Also emits a per-table poor-pruning signal consumed by A20.
A13
Spill Analyser
Is this a warehouse that is too small, or a query that is doing too much?
Lever Q06Phase 3Tier 1Veto provider
▸
Decision
Separates memory pressure caused by genuine data volume from memory pressure caused by a defect. This distinction is the single most consequential judgment in the library: the first calls for more warehouse, the second calls for a code change, and getting it backwards means paying double for a bug.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (bytes_spilled_to_local_storage, bytes_spilled_to_remote_storage, rows_produced, bytes_scanned, query_text, warehouse_size), the census.
Method
Treats remote spill as the red flag it is — it can slow a query by roughly an order of magnitude — and then diagnoses. The key statistic is the amplification ratio, rows_produced ÷ rows_scanned: a value far above 1 on a join means the intermediate result exploded, which is a duplicate join key or a missing predicate, not a sizing shortfall. Combined with text analysis for global sorts, unqualified DISTINCT and correlated subqueries, it classifies each spilling pattern as defect or volume. Only volume spill is allowed to become a sizing recommendation; defect spill becomes a rewrite ticket. It also tracks local spill trend, since growing local spill is the early warning that a workload is approaching the remote-spill cliff.
Fires when
Any remote spill on a pattern above the materiality floor; or local spill growing week over week on a top-100 pattern.
Guards
Bounded amplification inference: a legitimate one-to-many join also amplifies, so the analyser requires either a text signal or a week-over-week change in amplification before calling defect — a step change in amplification with unchanged SQL is upstream data, and that is a data-quality finding, not a cost one.
Emits
Classification per pattern with amplification and spill volumes, the rewrite recommendation or the sizing recommendation, $/year. Its veto output to A02 is mandatory: any warehouse with remote spill is blocked from downsizing.
A14
Result-Cache Leakage Analyser
Which repeated queries are paying full price for an answer Snowflake already had?
Lever Q02Phase 2–3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Finds work that should have been free. The result cache serves identical queries for 24 hours at zero warehouse credits, so every avoidable miss is a full-price re-execution of an answer that already existed.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (query_hash, query_parameterized_hash, query_text, percentage_scanned_from_cache, execution status), session and account parameter values, ACCESS_HISTORY for base-table change times.
Method
Three distinct leak classes, each with a different fix. (1) Disabled: USE_CACHED_RESULT = FALSE at account, user or session scope — usually a benchmark that was never reverted, and the cheapest finding in the library to remediate. (2) Defeated: patterns with high repetition, an unchanged base table, and near-zero cache service — then text-diff consecutive executions to isolate why: an injected CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(), a millisecond-resolution parameter, varying whitespace or column order from a generator. (3) Unreachable: semantically equivalent but textually different queries, which the cache cannot help with by design — handed to A15. The counterfactual prices the recoverable subset at full attributed credits, since a hit costs zero.
Fires when
Any scope with caching disabled. Or: >500 executions in 30 days, cache-served fraction <5%, base table unchanged between a material share of consecutive executions.
Guards
Genuine invalidation: if the base table changes between every execution the cache cannot serve it and there is nothing to fix — check base-table change times before claiming a saving. Deliberate disabling for benchmarking is legitimate; require a documented exemption rather than silently re-enabling.
Emits
Leak class, the specific defeating construct with a before/after text diff, executions, $/year. Class 1 is a one-line fix; classes 2 and 3 are pull requests against the generator or the model layer.
A15
Redundant Work Analyser
How many times is this organisation computing the same number from scratch?
Lever Q08Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Finds semantically duplicated computation that no cache can eliminate, because the queries differ in text while producing the same result. Six dashboards each aggregating the same events table every fifteen minutes is not a caching problem — it is a missing shared model, and it is one of the largest single recoveries available at this scale.
Reads
ACCESS_HISTORY (base_objects_accessed, columns_accessed), QUERY_HISTORY (text, attribution, tags), the census, dbt manifest where available.
Method
Fingerprints each pattern by what it reads and what it produces rather than by how it is written: the set of base objects, the columns touched, the grouping grain, the filter window and the aggregate functions used. Patterns sharing a fingerprint but differing in text are candidate duplicates. It then clusters them, sums the attributed credits across the cluster, and models the counterfactual as one shared materialization refreshed at the tightest freshness any member genuinely requires, plus cheap reads from it.
Fires when
Three or more distinct patterns share a fingerprint, combined credits exceed the materiality floor, and they are owned by more than one tag — the cross-team case is both the most expensive and the least likely to be noticed internally.
Guards
Fingerprint collision: similar shape is not identical semantics; findings are proposals with the member SQL attached for human confirmation, never auto-generated models. Freshness reconciliation: the shared model must serve the tightest real requirement in the cluster — if one member needs one-minute freshness, the saving is much smaller than the naive model suggests, and the analyser must say so rather than overstate.
Emits
Cluster of duplicate patterns with owners, combined $/year, proposed shared model grain and refresh cadence, and the residual saving after honouring the tightest freshness requirement.
A16

A17
Runaway Exposure Analyser
What is the most a single mistake could cost us today?
Lever Q01Phase 1Tier 2Auto-eligible
▸
Decision
Sets statement and queued timeouts per warehouse. Unusually, its primary output is not a saving but a quantified tail risk — and that framing is why it gets approved. "Please lower the timeout" is a debate about developer freedom; "this warehouse can lose $28,000 to one typo, today" is a decision.
Reads
QUERY_HISTORY (runtime distribution per warehouse, aborted and long-running statements), config snapshot for both timeout parameters, warehouse size and max clusters, rate card.
Method
Computes worst-case exposure as timeout_seconds × size_credit_rate × max_clusters × $/credit. The default is 172,800 seconds — two days — so a 4XL with the default carries five-figure exposure per statement. It then derives a defensible ceiling from the warehouse's own p99.9 runtime with generous headroom, keeping the bands from section 05 as a sanity floor: interactive 300–900s, ad-hoc 1,800s, batch 7,200–14,400s, ML 21,600s. It also backtests: how many legitimate statements in the last 90 days would the proposed ceiling have killed? If the answer is not zero, the proposal is wrong and it says so.
Fires when
Timeout unset, or set above 10× the warehouse's p99.9 runtime, or exposure above a dollar threshold. Queued timeout unset on any warehouse with MAX_CLUSTER_COUNT > 1.
Guards
Zero-kill backtest is mandatory — a timeout that would have killed real work is an incident waiting to happen, and one wrongful kill costs more political capital than the setting is worth. Seasonality: use a 90-day window so quarter-end long jobs are represented. Exception path: document how a team requests a higher ceiling, and publish it alongside the change.
Emits
Current exposure in dollars, recommended ceiling, backtest result, and the ALTER statements. Auto-eligible where the backtest kills zero historical statements.
A18
Metadata Chatter Analyser
Why is cloud services breaking through the 10% rebate?
Lever Q09Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Explains and reduces billed cloud-services credits. The line item is small — 1–3% — but it is diagnostically valuable out of proportion to its size: cloud services only becomes billable when it exceeds 10% of that day's warehouse credits, so a bill here means either enormous metadata traffic or unusually low warehouse usage, and both are worth knowing about.
Reads
METERING_DAILY_HISTORY (credits_used_cloud_services, credits_adjustment_cloud_services), QUERY_HISTORY (compilation_time, query type, bytes_scanned = 0 patterns), INFORMATION_SCHEMA and SHOW traffic by user.
Method
Identifies days where the rebate was exceeded, then decomposes the cloud-services load into three drivers: high-frequency metadata calls (catalog refreshes, SHOW loops, INFORMATION_SCHEMA polling — ranked by user and application), compilation-heavy queries (enormous generated SQL, deep view nesting, thousands of unioned branches, identified by compilation_time as a large fraction of elapsed), and zero-scan query storms. Fires when
Billed cloud services on more than a few days per month, or any single application accounting for a large share of metadata traffic, or a top-100 pattern where compilation exceeds a third of elapsed time.
Guards

Driver decomposition with named applications and users, the ratio trend, and targeted remediations with $/year.

C · Data layout & serverless analysers

Everything here bills at a 2× multiplier and is driven by table churn rather than query volume — which is why these features so often cost more than they save, and why nobody notices.

A19
Serverless Net-Value Analyser
Did this clustering key, search optimization config or materialized view pay for itself?
Levers S01 S02 S03Phase 1 & 3Tier 2Proposal
▸
Decision
Answers the question almost nobody asks: was this acceleration feature worth its maintenance cost? Automatic clustering, search optimization and materialized views all bill at a 2× multiplier driven by base-table churn, so a heavily-written and lightly-read table can generate enormous cost for no benefit at all.
Reads
AUTOMATIC_CLUSTERING_HISTORY, SEARCH_OPTIMIZATION_HISTORY, MATERIALIZED_VIEW_REFRESH_HISTORY, ACCESS_HISTORY (read events per object), QUERY_HISTORY for the workloads touching those objects, config for warehouse generation.
Method
A genuine benefit calculation, in two escalating tiers. Tier 1 (cheap, ships in Phase 1): maintenance credits > 0 and read events = 0 → unambiguous drop, no modelling required, and this alone is usually the fastest five-figure win in the estate. Tier 2 (Phase 3): for objects that are read, identify the query patterns served, estimate their cost without the feature — for clustering, the prune ratio at the table's unclustered depth; for search optimization, full-scan cost for the point lookups; for an MV, the base-query cost times execution count — and compare that avoided cost against actual maintenance credits. Net negative means drop.
Fires when
Zero reads with non-zero maintenance; or maintenance credits exceeding modelled avoided query credits; or cost-per-read above a threshold; or — specifically for Gen2 warehouses — a search optimization config whose access pattern is recurring selective point lookups that Snowflake Optima indexes automatically at no charge.
Guards
Latency versus cost: some of these exist to hold a user-facing SLA, not to save money. Honour an SLA tag and report the annual cost of that SLA rather than proposing removal — making the price visible is the useful act. Stacking: where clustering, SOS and an MV coexist on one table, model them jointly, because reclustering triggers maintenance of everything built on top. Optima is best-effort and must not be assumed to cover a hard latency requirement.
Emits
Per object: maintenance credits, reads, modelled avoided cost, net value, drop/keep/SLA-priced verdict, $/year.
A20
Clustering Health Analyser
Is this clustering key organizing the table, and does anyone filter on it?
Lever S01Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Evaluates key choice, where A19 evaluates whether clustering should exist at all. A well-maintained key on the wrong column costs continuously and prunes nothing.
Reads
SYSTEM$CLUSTERING_INFORMATION (average depth, overlap histogram), AUTOMATIC_CLUSTERING_HISTORY, ACCESS_HISTORY (columns actually filtered), QUERY_HISTORY (prune ratios on the table), table DDL.
Method
Two tests that must both pass. (1) Is the key working? Average depth near 1.0–1.2 is well-clustered; large values mean the key is not organizing the table — usually because it is high-cardinality or because random DML disorders it as fast as reclustering orders it. (2) Does the key match the workload? Compare key columns against the columns queries actually filter on, from ACCESS_HISTORY. A healthy key nobody filters on is pure cost. It also checks key width, since Snowflake permits up to 16 columns but practice favours 3–4, ordered lowest cardinality first.
Fires when
Depth persistently high with sustained reclustering spend; or key columns with near-zero filter frequency; or more than four key columns; or high-cardinality leading column; or high reclustering spend on a table with heavy random DML.
Guards
Query performance is the real indicator — depth is a diagnostic, not a goal. Do not propose a key change on a table whose queries are already pruning well. Churn awareness: on a table rewritten daily, no key survives; the honest recommendation is to drop clustering and change the write pattern, not to pick a better column.
Emits
Depth trend, key columns versus filtered columns, DML churn rate, and a change/drop/keep verdict with $/year.
A21
Dynamic Table Refresh Analyser
Is this table refreshing faster than anyone needs, or silently doing full refreshes?
Levers S04 S05Phase 2Tier 2Proposal
▸
Decision
Tunes TARGET_LAG and catches the silent fallback from incremental to full refresh. Two multipliers stack here — how often it refreshes, and how much work each refresh does — and a one-minute lag on a full-refresh table over a large base is among the most expensive configurations available in Snowflake, expressible in three words of DDL.
Reads
DYNAMIC_TABLE_REFRESH_HISTORY (duration, rows changed, refresh mode, refresh_mode_reason, failures), INFORMATION_SCHEMA.DYNAMIC_TABLES (lag, DAG edges), ACCESS_HISTORY (who reads it and how often), source-table arrival cadence.
Method
Three tests. (a) Mode: any table on FULL refresh with a large base is flagged with its refresh_mode_reason, since the cost difference against incremental is typically an order of magnitude and the fallback happens silently. (b) No-op rate: refreshes changing zero rows are pure waste — a high no-op rate means the lag is tighter than the source's arrival cadence. (c) Consumer demand: compare TARGET_LAG against how often the table is actually read; a one-minute lag feeding a dashboard opened twice a day is 700× over-refreshed. It also walks the DAG, because lag propagates upstream — one tight leaf forces every ancestor to refresh at least that often, so the analyser attributes the cost of a tight lag to the leaf that caused it.
Fires when
FULL mode on a base above a size floor; no-op rate > 80%; lag materially tighter than observed read cadence or source arrival cadence; or a leaf whose lag is inflating ancestor refresh cost.
Guards
Freshness is a product decision. The analyser proposes and prices; it never sets lag automatically. DAG attribution: report the cost at the leaf that set the tight lag, not spread across innocent ancestors, or the wrong team gets the ticket.
Emits
Per table: mode with reason, no-op rate, read cadence, DAG-propagated cost, proposed lag, $/year.
A22
Micro-Partition Health Analyser
Is this table physically laid out in a way that will cost us on every future query?
Levers S07 S08Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Finds tables whose physical layout permanently degrades pruning for everything downstream. This is a compounding tax: it is paid by every query against the table, forever, and it is invisible in any per-query view because each individual query just looks a bit slow.
Reads
TABLE_STORAGE_METRICS, SYSTEM$CLUSTERING_INFORMATION, PIPE_USAGE_HISTORY (file counts and sizes on ingest), QUERY_HISTORY prune ratios per table, COPY_HISTORY, external and Iceberg table refresh metadata.
Method
Correlates ingest shape with query outcome. Small files produce micro-partitions with wide overlapping value ranges, and pruning collapses. It computes average bytes per micro-partition, the table's natural ordering correlation against its most-filtered column, and the population-level prune ratio across all queries touching the table — then attributes poor pruning to layout where predicates are demonstrably prunable (A12 having already cleared the query side). Fires when
Average file size on ingest below 10MB; population prune ratio poor with prunable predicates; or metadata refresh cost high with a shallow partition layout.
Guards
Ordering: A12 must run first — blaming layout for what is actually a non-prunable WHERE clause sends an expensive rewrite to the wrong team. Remediation cost: rewriting a large table costs real credits; the finding must net that one-off cost against the ongoing saving and state the payback period, or it will be rejected on sight.
Emits
Table, layout diagnostics, attributed downstream query cost, proposed remediation (producer batching, compaction, reorder-on-rewrite) with payback period and $/year.

D · Pipeline analysers

Pipelines are where cost is created rather than consumed. A scheduling decision made once bills 8,760 times a year, which makes these analysers unusually high-leverage per finding.

A23
Ingest Economics Analyser
Is this pipeline using the right ingest method at the right file size?
Levers P01 P02Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Chooses between COPY, Snowpipe and Snowpipe Streaming, and drives the file-size conversation with upstream producers. Snowpipe's per-file charge is fixed regardless of file size, so cost per GB explodes as files shrink — the same terabyte can cost a thousand times more delivered as 100KB files than as 100MB files.
Reads
PIPE_USAGE_HISTORY (credits, bytes, files), COPY_HISTORY, Snowpipe Streaming client hours, QUERY_HISTORY for freshness of downstream consumption, arrival-time distribution per stage.

Computes credits per TB and average file size per pipe, then models the alternatives: the same volume via a batched COPY on a small warehouse, versus Snowpipe at 1.25× plus per-file charges, versus Streaming at 1× plus per-client-hour. Critically, it infers the actual latency requirement from downstream behaviour — when is the data first read after landing? — rather than accepting the stated one, because pipelines are routinely built for a freshness nobody consumes.
Fires when
Average file size < 10MB; or Snowpipe on a predictably-scheduled arrival pattern a COPY would serve; or Streaming clients billing per hour while sending no rows; or observed first-read latency far looser than pipeline freshness.
Guards
Producers are usually a different team, so the finding must be denominated in dollars per month and addressed to the producing system, not to the Snowflake owner who cannot change it. Latency inference is evidence, not proof — state it as an observation and ask for confirmation.
Emits
Per pipe: credits/TB, average file size, observed versus configured freshness, recommended method and batching target (100–250MB compressed), $/year.
A24
Task Execution Analyser
Are these tasks paying a 60-second minimum for five seconds of work?
Levers P03 P04 P05Phase 2Tier 2Partly auto-eligible
▸
Decision
Routes tasks between serverless and warehouse execution, tunes frequency, and stops failure loops. A warehouse task must resume a warehouse and therefore bills a 60-second minimum — so a 5-second task on a dedicated Medium running every minute pays a 12× overhead, 1,440 times a day.
Reads
TASK_HISTORY (duration, state, return value, schedule), SERVERLESS_TASK_HISTORY (credits), WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY for the warehouses tasks run on, stream freshness, task DDL for SUSPEND_TASK_AFTER_NUM_FAILURES and USER_TASK_MANAGED_INITIAL_WAREHOUSE_SIZE.
Method
Four tests. (a) Routing: warehouse tasks with median duration under 60 seconds are modelled on serverless, where billing is per actual execution second. (b) No-op rate: runs that change zero rows — gate them on SYSTEM$STREAM_HAS_DATA, which reduces the cost of a check to near nothing. (c) Failure loops: tasks with sustained high failure rates and SUSPEND_TASK_AFTER_NUM_FAILURES = 0, which can retry for weeks unnoticed — priced as wasted credits and flagged as an operational defect. (d) Batching: sibling tasks on the same warehouse with near-identical schedules, where one resume could serve many rather than each paying its own minimum.
Fires when
Median duration < 60s on a warehouse task; no-op rate > 80%; failure rate > 20% over 7 days; suspend-after-failures unset; or >5 sibling tasks on overlapping schedules.
Guards
Serverless is not universally cheaper — long, heavy, predictable tasks are often better on a right-sized warehouse, so the routing test is duration-conditioned, not a blanket rule. Graph awareness: never re-route a single task inside a dependency graph in isolation.
Emits
Per task: current model, recommended model, no-op rate, failure rate, $/year. The SUSPEND_TASK_AFTER_NUM_FAILURES fix is auto-eligible — it is a pure safety improvement with no downside.
A25
Materialization Strategy Analyser
Which full-refresh models are worth the engineering cost of converting to incremental?
Lever P06Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Ranks the incremental-conversion backlog. This is the largest single prize in most transformation stacks — a 3TB fact rebuilt hourly versus 5GB of change per hour is a ratio around 600:1 — and also the most expensive to act on, so ranking correctly matters more here than anywhere else.
Reads
dbt manifest and run_results or the query tag, QUERY_ATTRIBUTION_HISTORY per model, TABLE_STORAGE_METRICS for base sizes, ACCESS_HISTORY for change volume, STREAMS availability.
Method
Builds credits-per-model-run × runs-per-day to get annual cost per model. Estimates the incremental counterfactual from observed change volume as a fraction of total rows, applying a deliberately pessimistic efficiency factor since incremental logic carries merge overhead. Then — the part that makes it useful — it scores conversion difficulty: presence of a reliable watermark or stream, late-arriving data patterns, schema volatility, and whether the model has hard-to-reproduce non-deterministic logic. Model materialized as table, base above a size floor, frequency more than daily, and change ratio below a threshold.
Guards
Correctness is the real objection, and it is a legitimate one — full refresh is correct by construction. Every proposal must include a scheduled full-refresh reconciliation to detect divergence, or it will be refused by the owning team and should be. A26
Orchestrator Concurrency Analyser
Is this DAG paying three times over to finish ten percent sooner?
Lever P07Phase 3Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Sets orchestrator thread count against warehouse elasticity. Thread count determines how many clusters spin up, and because a DAG's completion time is usually bounded by one long model on the critical path rather than by aggregate throughput, extra parallelism frequently buys a marginal wall-clock gain at a multiple of the credits.
Reads

Where the critical path itself dominates, the honest recommendation is to fix that one model rather than to tune threads at all.
Fires when
Observed cluster-hours during the DAG window materially exceed the modelled requirement at the knee, or thread count sits well above it.
Guards
SLA windows: a batch that must complete before a business deadline has a wall-clock constraint that outranks cost — honour a declared deadline tag and optimize within it. Modelled, not measured: where possible, validate the knee with one real run at the proposed setting before adopting it.
Emits
Credits and wall-clock curve across thread counts, the knee, recommended setting, critical-path model if that is the true constraint, $/year.

E · Storage & lifecycle analysers

None of these are automatable. Their actions destroy data, and their rollback is "restore from Time Travel, if the window is still open" — which is not a rollback. Every finding here is a proposal to a named owner with a notice period.

A27
Storage Overhead Analyser
Which tables cost more to be able to recover than to hold?
Levers D01 D02 D03Phase 2Tier 4Never automated
▸
Decision
Sets Time Travel retention per layer and identifies tables that should be transient. Time Travel bills like active storage and is driven by churn, not size — so a 100GB table fully rewritten daily with 90-day retention holds roughly 9TB of history. Permanent tables add 7 non-configurable days of Fail-safe on top, for up to 97 days of recovery storage.
Reads
TABLE_STORAGE_METRICS (active_bytes, time_travel_bytes, failsafe_bytes, retained_for_clone_bytes), table type and retention DDL, ACCESS_HISTORY, stream lag, schema naming, tags.
Method
Computes the overhead ratio (time_travel + failsafe) ÷ active per table and prices the excess against the storage rate card. It then classifies reproducibility — whether the table is re-derivable from an upstream source, inferred from schema layer, lineage in ACCESS_HISTORY, and dbt materialization — because a reproducible table has no business carrying Fail-safe at all. Separately it finds stale streams holding MAX_DATA_EXTENSION_TIME_IN_DAYS open on high-churn tables, which silently extends retention beyond what anyone configured.
Fires when
Overhead ratio > 1.0 with material size; or a permanent table in a layer classified reproducible; or a stream unconsumed for 7+ days on a high-churn table.
Guards
Reducing retention immediately discards history beyond the new window — a one-way door. Named owner sign-off, a 14-day notice period, and a hard exclusion from automation, permanently. Compliance tags override everything; where a table is regulated, the analyser prices the requirement rather than challenging it, which is also the artifact that lets compliance be re-justified annually.
Emits
Per table: overhead ratio, reproducibility classification, proposed retention and table type, $/year, and the owner who must approve.

A30

Guards
Replay dependency: some teams deliberately retain raw files to replay a pipeline — a legitimate reason, so require confirmation and price the retention rather than assuming waste. Reproducibility check before proposing to drop a raw payload: confirm the flattened copy is genuinely lossless.
Emits
Stage inventory with ages and load status, storage reconciliation residual, duplicate-representation candidates, $/year, and the PURGE or scheduled REMOVE remediation.

F · Movement & AI analysers

Four separate meters that sit outside warehouse credits and outside most dashboards — which is exactly why they need dedicated analysers rather than a line on a chart.

A31
Replication RPO Fit Analyser
Are we replicating more, and more often, than any recovery objective requires?
Levers X01 X02Phase 5Tier 4Proposal
▸
Decision
Aligns replication scope and frequency to documented recovery objectives. Replication bills compute for the copy and per-TB egress across regions or clouds, and the usual finding is that whole databases are replicated every fifteen minutes against a documented 24-hour RPO.
Reads
REPLICATION_GROUP_USAGE_HISTORY, DATABASE_REPLICATION_USAGE_HISTORY, DATA_TRANSFER_HISTORY, replication group DDL and schedules, the documented RPO/RTO register, ACCESS_HISTORY in target regions.
Method
Two gaps. Scope gap: objects inside replication groups that carry no recovery objective in the register — cost priced per object. Frequency gap: replication cadence against documented RPO; cost scales with frequency × changed bytes, so a 15-minute cadence against a 24-hour RPO is roughly 96× the necessary spend on that dataset. It also checks target-region read activity: a replica nobody ever reads and no runbook ever fails over to is a candidate for removal entirely.
Fires when
Replicated objects absent from the RPO register; cadence materially tighter than documented RPO; or a target with no reads and no failover exercise on record.
Guards
DR is a business risk decision, not a cost decision. The analyser surfaces cost against stated objectives and never proposes reducing a documented objective — only aligning to it. Missing register: if no RPO register exists, that absence is the finding, because you cannot optimize against an undocumented requirement.
Emits
Per replication group: in-scope objects versus register, cadence versus RPO, target read activity, compute and egress split, $/year.
A32
Egress & Sharing Analyser
What are we paying to move data out, and who is spending our credits?
Levers X03 X04Phase 4–5Tier 4Proposal
▸
Decision
Reduces cross-region and cross-cloud egress, and caps reader-account consumption. Ingress is free; egress is charged per TB when data leaves the region or cloud — and reader accounts you provision for partners consume your compute.
Reads

Method

For reader accounts it computes consumption per account and, more importantly, checks whether a resource monitor with a hard suspend exists at all, since an unmonitored reader account is unbounded exposure on your budget.
Fires when
Recurring full uncompressed unloads; cross-region transfer where a share would serve; any reader account without a hard-suspend resource monitor; or reader consumption above an agreed allowance.
Guards
Contractual obligations: some egress is a commitment to a partner and cannot simply be optimized away — check the share agreement before proposing. The reader-monitor finding is a control gap, not a saving — report it that way, because framing it as savings invites an argument about whether the risk is real.
Emits
Egress attributed by cause with alternatives priced, reader-account consumption and monitor coverage, $/year and the control gaps.
A33
AI Token Economics Analyser
Are we using a frontier model to do a small model's job, on every row, every night?
Levers X05 X06Phase 2Tier 3Proposal
▸
Decision
Governs Cortex spend — model selection, prompt size and recomputation. This is the fastest-growing line in most estates and typically the least governed, going from nothing to a top-five item within two quarters. The per-million-token spread between small open models and frontier models is roughly 40×, multiplied by every row.
Reads

Method
Three tests. (a) Model fit: cost per thousand rows by model and function, with the same workload priced against cheaper models — a candidate list for the evaluation harness rather than an automatic switch, because accuracy is a product judgment. (b) Prompt efficiency: input tokens per row; a large static preamble repeated across hundreds of millions of rows is enormous and trivially fixable by moving static context out of the per-row payload. (c) Recomputation: the big one — detect rows re-scored whose inputs are unchanged, by comparing invocation counts against content-hash change rates. Persisting results keyed by content hash typically removes 95%+ of recurring AI spend, and it is the single highest-value finding in this family.
Fires when
Frontier model on a classification or extraction workload; input tokens per row above a threshold; or invocations materially exceeding the count of changed rows.
Guards
Never propose a model downgrade without an evaluation harness. The analyser produces the cost case and the candidate list; accuracy is measured separately on a labelled sample, and skipping that step is how you trade a cost problem for a quality incident. AI credits are a separate currency — flat-priced and decoupled from edition — so they need their own rate-card entry or every number here is wrong.
Emits
Per workload: model, tokens per row, cost per thousand rows, recomputation ratio, candidate cheaper models, and the persistence design with $/year.

G · Governance & meta analysers

These do not find savings directly. They make every other analyser trustworthy, keep savings from evaporating, and convert credits avoided into dollars realized — which is the part the rest of the program is judged on.

A35
Rate Card & Edition Analyser
What does a credit actually cost us, account by account?
Levers G01 G02Phase 0 & 5Tier 0Proposal
▸
Decision
Maintains the dollar conversion every other analyser depends on, and challenges edition and region placement. Edition is a direct multiplier on the price of every credit — roughly 1.5× Standard to Enterprise, 2× to Business Critical — with a further spread by cloud and region.
Reads
ORGANIZATION_USAGE.USAGE_IN_CURRENCY_DAILY, METERING_DAILY_HISTORY, account metadata (edition, region, cloud), contract terms.

Guards

Region moves are multi-quarter with migration costs the analyser must include, or the case will not survive contact with the architecture team.

The rate card itself — consumed by all 39 other analysers — plus effective $/credit by account, edition-fit findings and any placement business case.
A36
Commitment Burn-Down Analyser
Will the savings we are generating ever show up on an invoice?
Lever G03Phase 0 & 5Tier 4Finance-facing
▸
Decision
Tracks consumption against contracted capacity and forecasts to contract end. This is the analyser that prevents the program's most demoralising failure mode: engineering delivers a genuine 20% reduction, the invoice does not move because the capacity is prepaid, and the program is judged a failure in month nine.
Reads
USAGE_IN_CURRENCY_DAILY, contract terms (commitment amount, start and end, rollover provisions, on-demand overage rates), the applied-findings log from A40.
Method
Projects burn-down to contract end from trailing consumption, adjusted for the modelled effect of findings already applied and those in flight. It reports two distinct numbers that must never be merged: credits avoided (the engineering achievement, real and immediate) and dollars realized (the finance outcome, which under a commitment may not land until renewal). It alerts when projected burn-down deviates from plan in either direction — under-burn risks forfeiting prepaid capacity, over-burn risks on-demand overage rates.
Fires when
Projected burn-down deviates from plan by more than 5%; or contract end approaches with material unused capacity; or realized savings diverge from avoided credits by more than the commitment structure explains.
Guards
Never report avoided credits as dollars saved under a commitment. Doing it once destroys finance's trust in every number the program produces afterwards, and you do not get it back.
Emits
Burn-down curve and forecast to contract end, credits avoided versus dollars realized, and the negotiation pack input for renewal.
A37
Guardrail Coverage Analyser
What is currently uncapped?
Lever G04Phase 4Tier 4Auto-eligible (notify tier)
▸
Decision
Ensures every warehouse, account and reader has a resource monitor and a budget, and that thresholds are calibrated to something real. A monitor set far above realistic usage never fires and is functionally identical to having none.
Reads
Resource monitor and budget definitions, warehouse and account inventory, WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY for p95 monthly credits, notification integration configuration.
Method
Coverage diff plus threshold calibration. For every warehouse it computes a defensible quota from 90-day p95 monthly credits with headroom — roughly 130% — and compares it against the configured quota, flagging both absent monitors and monitors set so high they cannot fire. It also verifies that alerts route to the owning team's channel rather than a central inbox, since a monitor whose alert nobody reads is coverage on paper only.

Guards

Coverage matrix, proposed quotas with the calibration basis, routing gaps. Auto-eligible for creating monitors in notify-only mode; suspend actions always require approval.
A38
Attribution Coverage Analyser
How much of the bill can we actually assign to a human being?
Levers G05 Q03Phase 2Tier 0Gates everything
▸
Decision

A39
Configuration Drift Analyser
What has quietly changed back since we fixed it?
Cross-cuttingPhase 4Tier 0Auto-eligible (revert)
▸
Decision
Detects regression of applied findings and unreviewed new objects. Without it, an estate drifts roughly 20–30% of the way back toward baseline within a year — auto-suspend values creep up, new untagged warehouses appear, and nobody notices because each individual change is small and locally reasonable.
Reads
The daily SCD2 configuration snapshot (warehouses, tasks, pipes, dynamic tables, monitors, tags), the applied-findings log, WAREHOUSE_EVENTS_HISTORY, QUERY_HISTORY for the ALTER statements themselves.
Method
Diffs today's configuration against the last known-good state per object, then classifies each change: reverting an applied finding, a new object created outside the standard, or a legitimate approved change. Crucially it attributes the change to who ran the ALTER from query history, because drift is a conversation with a person, not a system. New objects are checked against creation-time standards — tags, auto-suspend, monitor, initially-suspended.
Fires when
Any applied finding's setting changes without an approval record; any new object missing mandatory tags or defaults; any warehouse whose config differs from the Terraform-declared state.
Guards
Drift is sometimes correct — the workload may genuinely have changed. The finding is a question addressed to the person who made the change, not an accusation, and legitimate reverts should update the analyser's baseline rather than being re-flagged weekly.
Emits
Drift events with actor, before and after, the original finding, and cost impact. Auto-eligible to revert only where the original finding was itself auto-applied and no approval record exists.
A40
Change Effect Analyser
Did the change actually save what we said it would?
Cross-cuttingPhase 4Tier 5Closes the loop
▸
Decision
Verifies every applied finding against its own prediction. This is the analyser that makes the library falsifiable, and it is the difference between a platform people trust and a platform people argue with. It also produces the "savings realized versus identified" metric the program is judged on.
Reads
The applied-findings log with predicted deltas, WAREHOUSE_METERING_HISTORY, QUERY_HISTORY before and after, workload-volume metrics for normalization, WAREHOUSE_EVENTS_HISTORY.
Method
For each applied change, compares actual metered cost over 7 and 28 days after against a normalized baseline — the pre-change period adjusted for workload volume, since raw before-and-after comparison is worthless when volume moved for unrelated reasons. It uses a difference-in-differences approach against comparable unchanged warehouses where one exists. It also measures the guard metrics the finding declared — p95 latency, queue time, failure rate — and triggers automatic rollback where those degrade beyond the agreed threshold. Prediction accuracy per detector is tracked over time and feeds back into that detector's confidence scoring.
Fires when
Actual saving deviates from predicted by more than 30%; any guard metric degrades beyond threshold (immediate rollback); or a detector's rolling prediction accuracy falls below its confidence tier's requirement.
Guards
Normalization is mandatory. A warehouse whose volume doubled the week after a downsize will show no saving and the finding will be wrongly judged a failure — and the engineer who applied it will not apply the next one. Attribution honesty: where two changes landed in the same window, report the combined effect and say so rather than splitting it by assumption.
Emits
Per finding: predicted versus actual, guard metric outcomes, verified status. Per detector: rolling prediction accuracy driving automation eligibility. Program-wide: savings realized versus identified.

Build order — the first eight

If you build these eight in this order, you can apply a real, reversible, evidenced change in week two and have the majority of Phase 1's savings in flight by week six. Everything else can wait.

  1. A35 Rate card — nothing has a dollar value without it, and dollars are what get changes approved.
  2. A11 Workload census — the shared input; also the artifact that first makes leadership believe you understand the estate.
  3. A39 Config snapshot — start capturing daily immediately, even before you use it. Every day you delay is a day of causation you can never reconstruct.
  4. A13 Spill — build the veto before the analyser it protects. This ordering is not optional.
  5. A04 Concurrency — the second veto, same reason.
  6. A10 Session liveness — the third veto, and cheap.
  7. A01 Idle tail — the first analyser that produces money, now safely.
  8. A02 Right-size — the largest Phase 1 prize, now with all three vetoes wired in.

The temptation is to build A01 and A02 first, because they are where the money is and they look self-contained. Resist it. An A02 built without A13 will, within its first month, recommend downsizing a warehouse that is spilling to remote storage — and the resulting incident will cost you the automation mandate for the rest of the year.

Operating model

The tool is maybe 40% of the outcome. The rest is who owns the number, what happens when it moves, and whether anyone is rewarded for making it smaller.

Federated, not central

A small platform team owns the tool, the taxonomy, the guardrails and the standards. Product teams own their own spend and their own reductions. Central teams that try to optimize other people's workloads run out of context and goodwill at roughly the same moment — usually about four months in.

Cadence

  • Daily: anomaly alerts routed to the owning team's channel.
  • Weekly: new findings triaged; top-10 review with the platform team.
  • Monthly: per-cost-centre showback; unit economics trend; savings realized vs plan.
  • Quarterly: re-baseline, re-tune every parameter band, review the buy-vs-build position.

Unit economics beat absolute spend

Absolute spend will grow if the business grows, and punishing growth turns the program into an obstacle. Track cost per unit of work — $/query, $/TB scanned, $/model run, $/dashboard load, $/1k rows scored, $/active user. A team that doubles its workload while holding cost per unit flat has done excellent work, and the reporting must say so.

Explicit anti-goals

  • Not a chargeback system. Showback creates the awareness that drives change; chargeback creates accounting theatre and incentives to hide workloads in someone else's warehouse. Revisit only after two quarters of trusted showback.
  • Not a query-approval gate. Slowing every analyst down to save credits costs the business more than it saves. Guardrails catch the pathological cases; they do not police normal work.
  • Not a headcount reduction exercise. The Phase 3 prize requires engineering time from product teams. A program that reads as cost-cutting will not get that time.
  • Not a replacement for capacity planning. The goal is efficiency per unit of work, not a smaller Snowflake. If the business needs to double its data volume, this program makes that affordable rather than preventing it.

SELECT warehouse_name,
       ROUND(SUM(credits_used_compute), 0)                     AS credits_90d,
       ROUND(SUM(credits_used_compute
             - credits_attributed_compute_queries), 0)          AS idle_credits_90d,
       ROUND(100*SUM(credits_used_compute - credits_attributed_compute_queries)
             /NULLIF(SUM(credits_used_compute),0), 1)             AS pct_idle,
       ROUND(SUM(credits_used_compute
             - credits_attributed_compute_queries) * 3.20 * 4, 0) AS annualized_idle_usd
FROM snowflake.account_usage.warehouse_metering_history
WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-90,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP())
GROUP BY 1
HAVING credits_90d > 100
ORDER BY idle_credits_90d DESC;

WITH spend AS (
  SELECT warehouse_name, SUM(credits_used_compute) AS credits,
         SUM(credits_used_compute - credits_attributed_compute_queries) AS idle
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.warehouse_metering_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP()) GROUP BY 1
), perf AS (
  SELECT warehouse_name,
         COUNT(*)                                            AS queries,
         AVG(query_load_percent)                             AS avg_load,
         SUM(bytes_spilled_to_remote_storage)                AS remote_spill,
         APPROX_PERCENTILE(queued_overload_time,0.95)/1000   AS p95_queue_s,
         APPROX_PERCENTILE(total_elapsed_time,0.95)/1000     AS p95_runtime_s,
         AVG(partitions_scanned/NULLIF(partitions_total,0))  AS avg_prune_ratio
  FROM snowflake.account_usage.query_history
  WHERE start_time >= DATEADD(day,-30,CURRENT_TIMESTAMP())
    AND warehouse_size IS NOT NULL GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT s.warehouse_name,
       ROUND(s.credits,0) AS credits_30d,
       ROUND(100*s.idle/NULLIF(s.credits,0),1) AS pct_idle,
       p.queries, ROUND(p.avg_load,1) AS avg_load_pct,
       ROUND(p.p95_queue_s,1) AS p95_queue_s,
       ROUND(p.p95_runtime_s,1) AS p95_runtime_s,
       ROUND(p.avg_prune_ratio,3) AS prune_ratio,
       ARRAY_CONSTRUCT_COMPACT(
         IFF(100*s.idle/NULLIF(s.credits,0) > 30, 'TUNE_AUTO_SUSPEND', NULL),
         IFF(p.avg_load < 25 AND p.remote_spill = 0 AND p.p95_queue_s < 2, 'DOWNSIZE', NULL),
         IFF(p.remote_spill > 0, 'FIX_QUERIES_SPILLING', NULL),
         IFF(p.p95_queue_s > 30, 'CONCURRENCY_NOT_SIZE', NULL),
         IFF(p.avg_prune_ratio > 0.8, 'POOR_PRUNING', NULL)
       ) AS actions
FROM spend s LEFT JOIN perf p USING (warehouse_name)
ORDER BY s.credits DESC LIMIT 25;
